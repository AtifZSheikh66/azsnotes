📐 Axiomatic Probability

The modern mathematical foundation of probability theory — Kolmogorov's axioms

Probability Theory · Unit I · Axiomatic Definition

1. The Need for an Axiomatic Foundation

The axiomatic approach to probability was developed by the Russian mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov in 1933 in his seminal work "Foundations of the Theory of Probability." It provides a rigorous mathematical foundation that unifies all previous interpretations of probability.

Before Kolmogorov, probability theory lacked a solid mathematical basis. The classical definition was limited to equally likely outcomes, and the empirical definition required repeatable experiments. Kolmogorov's axiomatic approach:

  • Places probability on firm mathematical ground using measure theory
  • Provides a unified framework for all probability interpretations
  • Makes probability a branch of mathematics rather than a purely empirical science
  • Allows for infinite sample spaces and continuous distributions
"Probability theory is a branch of mathematics that deals with the study of random phenomena. Its basic concepts are formulated axiomatically."
🎯

2. The Probability Space — The Mathematical Framework

A probability space is a mathematical triple \( (\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P) \) consisting of:
  • \( \Omega \) — the sample space (set of all possible outcomes)
  • \( \mathcal{F} \) — a sigma-algebra of events (subsets of \( \Omega \))
  • \( P \) — a probability measure (assigns probabilities to events)

📌 Components of a Probability Space

\[ \Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3, \ldots\} \]
\[ \mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\Omega) \quad \text{(collection of events)} \]
\[ P : \mathcal{F} \to [0, 1] \quad \text{(probability measure)} \]
📋

3. Kolmogorov's Three Axioms of Probability

Axiom 1: Non-negativity

\[ P(E) \geq 0 \quad \text{for every event } E \in \mathcal{F} \]

The probability of any event is a non-negative real number.

Axiom 2: Unit Measure (Normalization)

\[ P(\Omega) = 1 \]

The probability of the entire sample space (the certain event) is 1.

Axiom 3: Countable Additivity (Sigma-additivity)

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} E_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(E_i) \]

For any countable sequence of mutually exclusive events \(E_1, E_2, E_3, \ldots\) (i.e., \(E_i \cap E_j = \emptyset\) for \(i \neq j\)), the probability of their union equals the sum of their individual probabilities.

Note: For finite sample spaces, the third axiom simplifies to finite additivity:

\[ P(E_1 \cup E_2 \cup \cdots \cup E_n) = P(E_1) + P(E_2) + \cdots + P(E_n) \quad \text{for mutually exclusive } E_i \]
⚙️

4. Important Properties Derived from the Axioms

Property 1: Probability of the Impossible Event

\[ P(\emptyset) = 0 \]

Property 2: Complement Rule

\[ P(E^c) = 1 - P(E) \]

Property 3: Monotonicity

\[ \text{If } A \subseteq B \text{, then } P(A) \leq P(B) \]

Property 4: Finite Additivity (special case of Axiom 3)

\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \quad \text{if } A \cap B = \emptyset \]

Property 5: General Addition Rule (Union)

\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

Property 6: Inclusion-Exclusion Principle for Three Events

\[ P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) - P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C) \]

Property 7: Boole's Inequality (Union Bound)

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \]

Property 8: Continuity of Probability

\[ \text{If } A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq A_3 \subseteq \cdots \text{, then } P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n) \]
🎲

5. Classical Probability as a Special Case of Axiomatic Probability

Classical probability is a special case of axiomatic probability where the sample space is finite and all outcomes are equally likely.

📌 Classical Probability Derivation from Axioms

\[ \text{Let } \Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \ldots, \omega_n\} \text{ with } |\Omega| = n \]
\[ \text{By Axiom 1: } P(\{\omega_i\}) \geq 0 \quad \forall i \]
\[ \text{By Axiom 2: } \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(\{\omega_i\}) = 1 \]
\[ \text{If equally likely: } P(\{\omega_i\}) = \frac{1}{n} \quad \forall i \]
\[ \text{Then for any event } A \subseteq \Omega: P(A) = \sum_{\omega_i \in A} P(\{\omega_i\}) = \frac{|A|}{n} \]

📐 Classical Probability

  • Assumes equally likely outcomes
  • Finite sample space only
  • \( P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|\Omega|} \)
  • Based on symmetry/indifference

📊 Axiomatic Probability

  • No assumption of equally likely outcomes
  • Works for finite, countably infinite, and uncountable spaces
  • \( P \) is any measure satisfying three axioms
  • Classical is a special case when \( P \) is uniform
📖 Example: Classical Probability as a Special Case
Consider rolling a fair die. Sample space: \( \Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\} \).
Classical approach: \( P(1) = P(2) = \cdots = P(6) = \frac{1}{6} \) (by symmetry).
Axiomatic approach: Define \( P(\{i\}) = \frac{1}{6} \). Then:
  • Axiom 1: \( \frac{1}{6} \geq 0 \) ✓
  • Axiom 2: \( \sum_{i=1}^6 \frac{1}{6} = 1 \) ✓
  • Axiom 3: For any mutually exclusive events, additivity holds ✓
Thus the classical assignment satisfies all three axioms, making classical probability a valid probability measure within the axiomatic framework.
🔍

6. Empirical and Subjective Probabilities in the Axiomatic Framework

📊 Empirical (Statistical) Probability

Within the axiomatic framework, empirical probability is viewed as an estimate of the true probability measure \(P\). The Law of Large Numbers ensures that the empirical relative frequency converges to \(P(E)\).

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\text{Number of times } E \text{ occurs}}{n} = P(E) \]

🧠 Subjective (Bayesian) Probability

Subjective probabilities (degrees of belief) also satisfy Kolmogorov's axioms if they are coherent. A person's subjective probability assignments must obey the axioms to avoid Dutch book (sure loss).

\[ \text{Coherence} \iff \text{Satisfies Axioms 1-3} \]

Unification: The beauty of the axiomatic approach is that it provides a single mathematical framework that encompasses all valid probability assignments — whether derived from classical symmetry, empirical frequency, or subjective belief — as long as they satisfy the three axioms.

7. Axiomatic Probability for Infinite Sample Spaces

The axiomatic approach is essential for dealing with infinite sample spaces, where classical probability fails. For example, consider selecting a random real number between 0 and 1.

📌 Uniform Distribution on [0, 1]

\[ \Omega = [0, 1] \quad \text{(uncountably infinite)} \]
\[ P([a, b]) = b - a \quad \text{for } 0 \leq a \leq b \leq 1 \]
\[ P(\{\omega\}) = 0 \quad \text{for any single point } \omega \]
💡 Key Insight: In continuous sample spaces, individual outcomes can have probability zero while intervals have positive probability. This is perfectly valid within the axiomatic framework but cannot be captured by classical probability.
🔢

8. Countable Additivity vs. Finite Additivity

Kolmogorov's third axiom requires countable additivity (sigma-additivity), which is stronger than finite additivity. This distinction is crucial for infinite sample spaces.

Finite Additivity

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \quad \text{for mutually exclusive } A_i \]

Valid for finite collections of events.

Countable Additivity (Sigma-additivity)

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i) \quad \text{for mutually exclusive } A_i \]

Required for infinite collections; essential for continuous distributions and limit theorems.

📖 Why Countable Additivity Matters:
Consider choosing a random integer. If we only require finite additivity, we could assign zero probability to each integer but still have \(P(\mathbb{Z}) = 1\). This leads to paradoxes. Countable additivity prevents such inconsistencies by requiring: \[ 1 = P(\mathbb{Z}) = P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} \{i\} \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(\{i\}) \] Thus the sum of probabilities over all integers must be 1, which is impossible if each has probability zero. This shows that a uniform distribution over a countable infinite set is impossible in the axiomatic framework.

9. Worked Examples

Example 1: Verifying Axioms
Let \( \Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4\} \). Define \( P(\{1\}) = 0.1, P(\{2\}) = 0.2, P(\{3\}) = 0.3, P(\{4\}) = 0.4 \). Verify the axioms.

Solution:
Axiom 1: All probabilities ≥ 0 ✓
Axiom 2: \( \sum P = 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.3 + 0.4 = 1.0 \) ✓
Axiom 3: For any mutually exclusive events, additivity holds (by construction) ✓
This is a valid probability measure.
Example 2: Classical as Axiomatic
Show that the classical probability definition satisfies Kolmogorov's axioms.

Solution:
Let \( \Omega = \{\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_n\} \) with \( P(\{\omega_i\}) = \frac{1}{n} \).
Axiom 1: \( \frac{1}{n} \geq 0 \) ✓
Axiom 2: \( \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{n} = 1 \) ✓
Axiom 3: If \( A \) and \( B \) are mutually exclusive, \( |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| \), so: \[ P(A \cup B) = \frac{|A \cup B|}{n} = \frac{|A| + |B|}{n} = \frac{|A|}{n} + \frac{|B|}{n} = P(A) + P(B) \] For more than two disjoint events, additivity extends by induction.
Example 3: Using Axioms to Find Probability
Let \( A \) and \( B \) be events with \( P(A) = 0.4 \), \( P(B) = 0.6 \), and \( P(A \cup B) = 0.8 \). Find \( P(A \cap B) \).

Solution:
Using the addition rule (derived from the axioms): \[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \] \[ 0.8 = 0.4 + 0.6 - P(A \cap B) \] \[ 0.8 = 1.0 - P(A \cap B) \] \[ P(A \cap B) = 0.2 \]

10. Practice Problems

Problem 1: A probability measure is defined on a sample space with three outcomes. \( P(a) = 0.2 \), \( P(b) = 0.5 \). What is \( P(c) \)?

Show Solution

By Axiom 2: \( P(a) + P(b) + P(c) = 1 \Rightarrow 0.2 + 0.5 + P(c) = 1 \Rightarrow P(c) = 0.3 \)

Problem 2: For events \( A \) and \( B \), prove that \( P(A \cup B) + P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) \).

Show Solution

By the addition rule: \( P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \). Rearranging gives \( P(A \cup B) + P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) \).

Problem 3: Is it possible to have \( P(A) = 0.6 \), \( P(B) = 0.5 \), and \( P(A \cap B) = 0.2 \)? Explain.

Show Solution

\( P(A \cup B) = 0.6 + 0.5 - 0.2 = 0.9 \). Since probabilities must be ≤ 1, and 0.9 ≤ 1, this is possible. The only constraint is that \( P(A \cap B) \leq \min(P(A), P(B)) = 0.5 \), and 0.2 ≤ 0.5 ✓.

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Kolmogorov's Three Axioms: Non-negativity, Unit Measure, and Countable Additivity form the foundation of modern probability theory
  • Axiomatic probability provides a rigorous mathematical framework applicable to any valid probability assignment
  • Classical probability is a special case where \( P(\{\omega_i\}) = \frac{1}{n} \) for all outcomes in a finite sample space
  • Empirical and subjective probabilities also fit within the axiomatic framework as long as they satisfy the axioms
  • The axioms allow probability theory to handle finite, countably infinite, and uncountably infinite sample spaces
  • Countable additivity (not just finite additivity) is essential for infinite sample spaces and limit theorems
