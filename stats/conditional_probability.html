📊 Conditional Probability

The probability of an event given that another event has occurred — updating beliefs with partial information

Probability Theory · Unit II · Conditional Probability
❓

1. What is Conditional Probability?

Conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring given that another event has already occurred. It allows us to update our probability assessments in light of new information.

In many real-world situations, we are not interested in the absolute probability of an event, but rather the probability of an event given that we know something else has happened. For example:

  • What is the probability that a person has a disease given that they tested positive?
  • What is the probability that a card drawn from a deck is an ace given that it is a heart?
  • What is the probability that it will rain given that the sky is cloudy?

The notation \( P(A \mid B) \) is read as "the probability of A given B."

📐

2. Formal Definition

📌 Definition of Conditional Probability

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad P(B) > 0 \]

Where:

  • \( P(A \mid B) \) is the conditional probability of A given B
  • \( P(A \cap B) \) is the joint probability of A and B occurring together
  • \( P(B) \) is the marginal probability of B

The condition \( P(B) > 0 \) ensures we are not dividing by zero — we cannot condition on an impossible event.

📝 Intuition Behind the Definition:

Step 1: When we know that B has occurred, the sample space is reduced from \( \Omega \) to \( B \).
Step 2: Within this reduced sample space, the probability of A is proportional to the probability of \( A \cap B \).
Step 3: To ensure the total probability of the new sample space B is 1, we divide by \( P(B) \).
Step 4: Thus, \( P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \) is the natural definition.
🔧

3. Properties of Conditional Probability

📌 Property 1: Conditional Probability is a Probability Measure

For a fixed event B with \( P(B) > 0 \), the function \( P(\cdot \mid B) \) satisfies Kolmogorov's axioms:

  • Non-negativity: \( P(A \mid B) \geq 0 \) for any event A
  • Unit measure: \( P(\Omega \mid B) = 1 \)
  • Additivity: If \( A_1, A_2, \ldots \) are mutually exclusive, then \( P(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \mid B) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i \mid B) \)

📝 Verification of Properties:

Non-negativity: Since \( P(A \cap B) \geq 0 \) and \( P(B) > 0 \), their quotient is non-negative.
Unit measure: \( P(\Omega \mid B) = \frac{P(\Omega \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B)}{P(B)} = 1 \).
Additivity: For disjoint \( A_i \), \( ( \bigcup_i A_i ) \cap B = \bigcup_i (A_i \cap B) \) are also disjoint. Therefore,
\[ P\left( \bigcup_i A_i \mid B \right) = \frac{P( \bigcup_i (A_i \cap B) )}{P(B)} = \frac{\sum_i P(A_i \cap B)}{P(B)} = \sum_i P(A_i \mid B) \]

📌 Property 2: Complement Rule for Conditional Probability

\[ P(A^c \mid B) = 1 - P(A \mid B) \]
Proof: Since \( A \) and \( A^c \) are mutually exclusive and \( A \cup A^c = \Omega \),
\[ P(A \mid B) + P(A^c \mid B) = P(\Omega \mid B) = 1 \]
Hence, \( P(A^c \mid B) = 1 - P(A \mid B) \).

📌 Property 3: Multiplication Rule

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A) \]

This follows directly from the definition of conditional probability and is one of the most important formulas in probability.

📌 Corollary 3.1 (Multiplication Rule for n events):
\[ P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdot P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1}) \]
✖️

4. Multiplication Rule for n Events

📌 Theorem (General Multiplication Rule)

\[ P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdot P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1}) \]

📝 Proof by Induction:

Base case (n = 2): \( P(A_1 \cap A_2) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \) by definition.
Inductive hypothesis: Assume the formula holds for n events.
Inductive step: For n+1 events,
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n+1} A_i \right) = P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \cdot P\left( A_{n+1} \mid \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \]

By the inductive hypothesis, the first term expands to the product of n conditional probabilities. Multiplying by the last term gives the product of n+1 conditional probabilities.

📖 Example: Three cards are drawn without replacement from a standard deck. Find the probability of drawing three aces in a row.
\[ P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdot P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) = \frac{4}{52} \cdot \frac{3}{51} \cdot \frac{2}{50} = \frac{24}{132600} = \frac{1}{5525} \approx 0.000181 \]
📊

5. Law of Total Probability

📌 Theorem (Law of Total Probability)

Let \( B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n \) form a partition of the sample space \( \Omega \) (i.e., they are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, with \( P(B_i) > 0 \) for all i). Then for any event A,

\[ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i) \]

📝 Proof:

Step 1: Since \( B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n \) partition \( \Omega \), we have:
\[ A = A \cap \Omega = A \cap \left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} B_i \right) = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} (A \cap B_i) \]
Step 2: The events \( A \cap B_i \) are mutually exclusive because the \( B_i \) are disjoint.
Step 3: By Axiom 3 (countable additivity):
\[ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \cap B_i) \]
Step 4: By the multiplication rule, \( P(A \cap B_i) = P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i) \).
Step 5: Therefore:
\[ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i) \]
📖 Example: A factory has three machines producing 20%, 30%, and 50% of the total output, with defect rates 1%, 2%, and 3% respectively. Find the probability that a randomly selected product is defective.
Solution: Let D be the event that a product is defective. Then:
\[ P(D) = P(D \mid M_1)P(M_1) + P(D \mid M_2)P(M_2) + P(D \mid M_3)P(M_3) \]
\[ P(D) = 0.01 \times 0.2 + 0.02 \times 0.3 + 0.03 \times 0.5 = 0.002 + 0.006 + 0.015 = 0.023 \]
🧠

6. Bayes' Theorem

📌 Theorem (Bayes' Theorem)

\[ P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_j) \cdot P(B_j)} \]

Where \( B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n \) form a partition of the sample space.

For two events, Bayes' theorem simplifies to:

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A \mid B)P(B) + P(A \mid B^c)P(B^c)} \]

📝 Proof:

Step 1: From the definition of conditional probability:
\[ P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B_i)}{P(A)} \]
Step 2: Apply the multiplication rule to the numerator:
\[ P(A \cap B_i) = P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i) \]
Step 3: Apply the Law of Total Probability to the denominator:
\[ P(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_j) \cdot P(B_j) \]
Step 4: Combining gives the result:
\[ P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_j) P(B_j)} \]
📖 Example (Medical Test): A disease affects 1 in 1000 people. A test has 99% sensitivity and 95% specificity. If a person tests positive, what is the probability they actually have the disease?
Let D = "has the disease", T = "tests positive". Then:
\[ P(D \mid T) = \frac{P(T \mid D)P(D)}{P(T \mid D)P(D) + P(T \mid D^c)P(D^c)} \]
\[ = \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.05 \times 0.999} = \frac{0.00099}{0.00099 + 0.04995} = \frac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx 0.0194 \]

Thus, even with a positive test, only about 1.94% of people actually have the disease — highlighting the importance of considering base rates.

7. Worked Examples

Example 1: Two Dice
Two fair dice are rolled. Find the probability that the sum is 8 given that the first die shows a 3.

Solution: Let A = "sum is 8" and B = "first die shows 3".
\( P(B) = \frac{1}{6} \). A ∩ B occurs only when (3,5) gives sum 8. So \( P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{36} \).
Thus, \( P(A \mid B) = \frac{1/36}{1/6} = \frac{1}{6} \).
Example 2: Card Draw (Without Replacement)
Two cards are drawn from a standard deck without replacement. Find the probability that the second card is an ace given that the first card is an ace.

Solution: After drawing the first ace, 3 aces remain among 51 cards.
\( P(\text{second ace} \mid \text{first ace}) = \frac{3}{51} = \frac{1}{17} \approx 0.0588 \).
Example 3: Conditional Probability Tree
In a certain population, 40% have blue eyes and 30% have blonde hair. Among those with blue eyes, 50% have blonde hair. Find the probability that a randomly selected person has blue eyes given that they have blonde hair.

Solution: \( P(B) = 0.4 \) (blue eyes), \( P(H) = 0.3 \) (blonde hair), \( P(H \mid B) = 0.5 \).
\( P(B \cap H) = P(H \mid B)P(B) = 0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.2 \).
Then \( P(B \mid H) = \frac{P(B \cap H)}{P(H)} = \frac{0.2}{0.3} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.6667 \).

8. Practice Problems

Problem 1: A box contains 3 red balls and 5 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. What is the probability that the second ball is red given that the first ball was blue?

Show Solution

After drawing a blue ball, there are 3 red and 4 blue balls left (total 7). \( P(\text{second red} \mid \text{first blue}) = \frac{3}{7} \approx 0.4286 \).

Problem 2: A family has two children. Given that at least one is a boy, what is the probability that both are boys? (Assume equal probability for boy/girl)

Show Solution

Sample space: {BB, BG, GB, GG}. Given at least one boy: {BB, BG, GB}. Only one favorable outcome (BB). \( P = \frac{1}{3} \).

Problem 3: In a class, 60% are male, 40% are female. 80% of males and 70% of females passed the exam. A student is chosen at random. What is the probability that they are male given that they passed the exam?

Show Solution

\( P(M) = 0.6 \), \( P(F) = 0.4 \), \( P(Pass \mid M) = 0.8 \), \( P(Pass \mid F) = 0.7 \).
\( P(Pass) = 0.8 \times 0.6 + 0.7 \times 0.4 = 0.48 + 0.28 = 0.76 \).
\( P(M \mid Pass) = \frac{0.48}{0.76} = \frac{48}{76} = \frac{12}{19} \approx 0.6316 \).

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Conditional Probability: \( P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \), defined only when \( P(B) > 0 \)
  • Multiplication Rule: \( P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) P(B) = P(B \mid A) P(A) \)
  • Law of Total Probability: \( P(A) = \sum_{i} P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i) \), where \( \{B_i\} \) partition the sample space
  • Bayes' Theorem: Updates prior probabilities with new evidence: \( P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) P(B)}{P(A)} \)
  • Conditional probability satisfies all probability axioms for a fixed conditioning event
