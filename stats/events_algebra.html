📋 Events & Algebra of Events

Understanding subsets of sample space and operations combining them

Probability Theory · Unit I · Core Concepts
❓

1. What is an Event?

An event is a subset of the sample space \( \Omega \). It is a collection of one or more outcomes (sample points).

When a random experiment is performed, an event is said to occur if the observed outcome is an element of that event.

📌 Important Distinction:
  • An outcome (or sample point) is a single result of an experiment.
  • An event is a set of outcomes — it can contain one outcome or many outcomes.

Examples of Events

Experiment: Rolling a fair die

Sample Space: \( \Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\} \)

  • Event A = "Rolling an even number" = \( \{2, 4, 6\} \)
  • Event B = "Rolling a number greater than 4" = \( \{5, 6\} \)
  • Event C = "Rolling a prime number" = \( \{2, 3, 5\} \)
  • Event D = "Rolling a 1" = \( \{1\} \) (simple/ elementary event)

Experiment: Tossing two coins

Sample Space: \( \Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\} \)

  • Event E = "At least one head" = \( \{HH, HT, TH\} \)
  • Event F = "Both same faces" = \( \{HH, TT\} \)
  • Event G = "Exactly one tail" = \( \{HT, TH\} \)
⭐

2. Special Types of Events

🔵 Simple (Elementary) Event

An event containing exactly one outcome: \( A = \{\omega\} \)

Example: Rolling a 4 on a die: \( \{4\} \)

🟢 Compound Event

An event containing two or more outcomes.

Example: Rolling an even number: \( \{2, 4, 6\} \)

🟠 Certain (Sure) Event

The event that always occurs: \( \Omega \) (the sample space itself).

\( P(\Omega) = 1 \)

🟣 Impossible Event

The event that never occurs: \( \emptyset \) (empty set).

\( P(\emptyset) = 0 \)

💡 Note: Every outcome is an event (simple event), but not every event is a single outcome — events can be any subset of the sample space, including the entire space and the empty set.
🔢

3. Algebra of Events

Just as we can combine sets using operations like union, intersection, and complement, we can combine events using similar operations. The algebra of events is a Boolean algebra — it follows the same rules as set algebra.

If \( A \) and \( B \) are events (subsets of \( \Omega \)), then:
  • \( A \cup B \) (A or B) is an event
  • \( A \cap B \) (A and B) is an event
  • \( A^c \) (not A) is an event
  • \( A \setminus B \) (A but not B) is an event
⚙️

4. Fundamental Operations on Events

📌 Union — Event "A or B"

The union of two events \( A \) and \( B \), denoted \( A \cup B \), is the event consisting of all outcomes that are in \( A \) or in \( B \) (or in both).

\[ A \cup B = \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A \text{ or } \omega \in B\} \]

This event occurs if at least one of the events \( A \) or \( B \) occurs.

Example: Die roll. \( A = \{2, 4, 6\} \) (even), \( B = \{5, 6\} \) (>4).
\( A \cup B = \{2, 4, 5, 6\} \)

📌 Intersection — Event "A and B"

The intersection of two events \( A \) and \( B \), denoted \( A \cap B \), is the event consisting of all outcomes that are in both \( A \) and \( B \).

\[ A \cap B = \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A \text{ and } \omega \in B\} \]

This event occurs if both events \( A \) and \( B \) occur simultaneously.

Example: Die roll. \( A = \{2, 4, 6\} \), \( B = \{5, 6\} \).
\( A \cap B = \{6\} \)

📌 Complement — Event "not A"

The complement of an event \( A \), denoted \( A^c \) or \( A' \) or \( \bar{A} \), is the event consisting of all outcomes in \( \Omega \) that are not in \( A \).

\[ A^c = \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \notin A\} = \Omega \setminus A \]

This event occurs if \( A \) does not occur. Complement of the sample space is the impossible event: \( \Omega^c = \emptyset \).

Example: Die roll. \( A = \{2, 4, 6\} \) (even).
\( A^c = \{1, 3, 5\} \) (odd)

📌 Difference — Event "A but not B"

The difference of events \( A \) and \( B \), denoted \( A \setminus B \) or \( A - B \), is the event consisting of outcomes in \( A \) that are not in \( B \).

\[ A \setminus B = \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A \text{ and } \omega \notin B\} = A \cap B^c \]

This event occurs if \( A \) occurs but \( B \) does not occur.

Example: Die roll. \( A = \{2, 4, 6\} \), \( B = \{5, 6\} \).
\( A \setminus B = \{2, 4\} \)
🔗

5. Relationships Between Events

📌 Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint) Events

Two events \( A \) and \( B \) are mutually exclusive if they cannot occur at the same time — their intersection is empty.

\[ A \cap B = \emptyset \]

Example: Rolling a die: \( A = \{2, 4, 6\} \), \( B = \{1, 3, 5\} \). No outcome is both even and odd.

📌 Exhaustive Events

Events \( A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n \) are collectively exhaustive if their union equals the entire sample space.

\[ \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i = \Omega \]

At least one of the events must occur.

Example: \( A = \{1, 2, 3\} \), \( B = \{4, 5, 6\} \) — together they cover all outcomes.

📌 Partition of Sample Space

A collection of events forms a partition if they are:

  • Mutually exclusive (\( A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset \) for \( i \neq j \))
  • Collectively exhaustive (\( \bigcup_i A_i = \Omega \))

\[ \Omega = A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots \cup A_n, \quad A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset \ (i \neq j) \]

📌 Subset Relationship

If all outcomes of event \( A \) are also in event \( B \), we write \( A \subseteq B \).

When \( A \subseteq B \), the occurrence of \( A \) implies the occurrence of \( B \).

Example: \( A = \{2, 4\} \), \( B = \{2, 4, 6\} \). Rolling a 2 or 4 implies rolling an even number.

6. Laws of Algebra of Events

The algebra of events follows the same laws as set algebra. These laws help simplify complex event expressions.

Commutative Laws

\[ A \cup B = B \cup A \] \[ A \cap B = B \cap A \]

The order of events does not matter for union or intersection.

Associative Laws

\[ (A \cup B) \cup C = A \cup (B \cup C) \] \[ (A \cap B) \cap C = A \cap (B \cap C) \]

Distributive Laws

\[ A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C) \] \[ A \cup (B \cap C) = (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C) \]

These are analogous to the distributive law in arithmetic: \( a \times (b + c) = a \times b + a \times c \).

De Morgan's Laws

\[ (A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c \] \[ (A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c \]

The complement of a union is the intersection of complements. The complement of an intersection is the union of complements.

Example: The complement of "rolling an even OR a prime number" is "rolling a number that is NEITHER even NOR prime" (i.e., odd AND not prime). For die: Even = {2,4,6}, Prime = {2,3,5}. Union = {2,3,4,5,6}. Complement = {1}. And indeed {1} is odd AND not prime.

Identity Laws

\[ A \cup \emptyset = A, \quad A \cap \Omega = A \] \[ A \cup \Omega = \Omega, \quad A \cap \emptyset = \emptyset \]

Complement Laws

\[ A \cup A^c = \Omega \] \[ A \cap A^c = \emptyset \] \[ (A^c)^c = A \]

Idempotent Laws

\[ A \cup A = A \] \[ A \cap A = A \]

7. Venn Diagram Representation

Venn diagrams provide a visual way to understand relationships between events. The sample space \( \Omega \) is represented as a rectangle, and events are represented as circles (or other shapes) within it.

⋃ — Union
Circles A and B (shaded area)
∩ — Intersection
Overlap region shaded
Aᶜ — Complement
Everything outside A shaded
A \ B — Difference
A only (not B) shaded
💡 Venn diagrams are extremely useful for solving probability problems involving two or three events. The entire rectangle (sample space) has total probability 1, and areas represent probabilities.

8. Worked Examples

Example 1: Two Dice Roll
Consider the experiment of rolling two fair dice. Let:
  • \( A \) = "Sum is 7"
  • \( B \) = "First die shows 4"
Find \( A \cup B \), \( A \cap B \), and \( A \setminus B \).

Solution: Sample space has 36 outcomes.
\( A = \{(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)\} \)
\( B = \{(4,1), (4,2), (4,3), (4,4), (4,5), (4,6)\} \)
\( A \cap B = \{(4,3)\} \) (only the outcome where first die is 4 AND sum is 7)
\( A \cup B = A \cup B \) — all outcomes in A or B (11 outcomes)
\( A \setminus B = \{(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (5,2), (6,1)\} \) (A outcomes where first die is not 4)
Example 2: Drawing Cards
One card is drawn from a standard 52-card deck. Define:
  • \( A \) = "Card is a heart"
  • \( B \) = "Card is a face card (J, Q, K)"
Describe \( A \cap B \), \( A \cup B \), and \( A^c \).

Solution:
\( A \cap B \) = "Heart face card" — {Jack of Hearts, Queen of Hearts, King of Hearts} (3 cards)
\( A \cup B \) = "Heart or face card" — 13 hearts + 12 face cards - 3 overlap = 22 cards
\( A^c \) = "Not a heart" — 39 cards (spades, clubs, diamonds)

9. Check Your Understanding

Question 1: For a single die roll, let \( A = \{1, 2, 3\} \) and \( B = \{3, 4, 5\} \). Find \( A \cup B \), \( A \cap B \), \( A \setminus B \), and \( A^c \).

Show Answer

\( A \cup B = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\} \), \( A \cap B = \{3\} \), \( A \setminus B = \{1, 2\} \), \( A^c = \{4, 5, 6\} \)

Question 2: If \( A \) and \( B \) are mutually exclusive events, what is \( A \cap B \)? What is \( P(A \cap B) \)?

Show Answer

\( A \cap B = \emptyset \), and \( P(\emptyset) = 0 \).

Question 3: Using De Morgan's law, simplify \( (A^c \cap B^c)^c \).

Show Answer

By De Morgan's law, \( (A^c \cap B^c)^c = A \cup B \).

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • An event is any subset of the sample space \( \Omega \)
  • The algebra of events uses set operations: union (\( \cup \)), intersection (\( \cap \)), complement (\( ^c \)), and difference (\( \setminus \))
  • Mutually exclusive events have \( A \cap B = \emptyset \)
  • Exhaustive events satisfy \( A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots \cup A_n = \Omega \)
  • De Morgan's laws relate complements of unions and intersections
  • The algebra of events forms a Boolean algebra with the same laws as set theory
