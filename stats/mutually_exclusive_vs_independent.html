⚖️ Mutually Exclusive vs Independent Events

Understanding the critical difference — two concepts often confused by students

Probability Theory · Unit II · Event Relationships
📜

1. Introduction

Two of the most important and frequently confused concepts in probability theory are mutually exclusive (disjoint) events and independent events. Many students mistakenly believe these concepts are related or interchangeable, but they are fundamentally different.

This page clarifies the distinction, proves the relationship between them, and provides visual intuition using Venn diagrams.

📚

2. Definitions

📌 Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint) Events

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are said to be mutually exclusive (or disjoint) if they cannot occur simultaneously. That is:

\[ A \cap B = \emptyset \quad \text{and consequently} \quad P(A \cap B) = 0 \]

Mutually exclusive events represent outcomes that are incompatible with each other.

📌 Independent Events

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are said to be independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the occurrence of the other. That is:

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B) \]

If \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\), this is equivalent to:

\[ P(A \mid B) = P(A) \quad \text{and} \quad P(B \mid A) = P(B) \]

4. Theorem 1: Mutually Exclusive Events with Positive Probabilities are Always Dependent

📌 Statement

If \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive (disjoint) events and \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\), then \(A\) and \(B\) are dependent. In other words, two mutually exclusive events (both with positive probability) cannot be independent.

📝 Proof:

Step 1 — State the given conditions:

Given that \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, we have:

\[ A \cap B = \emptyset \quad \Rightarrow \quad P(A \cap B) = 0 \]

Also given: \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\).

Step 2 — If events were independent, what would we expect?

If \(A\) and \(B\) were independent, the definition of independence would require:

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B) \]
Step 3 — Compare the two expressions:

From Step 1, \(P(A \cap B) = 0\). From Step 2, if independent, \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) > 0\) (since both \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\)).

\[ 0 = P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) > 0 \]
Step 4 — Contradiction:

This leads to a contradiction because \(0 > 0\) is false. Therefore, the assumption that mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are independent is false.

Step 5 — Conclusion:

Hence, mutually exclusive events with \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\) must be dependent.

\[ \boxed{P(A \cap B) = 0 \neq P(A)P(B) \quad \text{when } P(A)>0,\; P(B)>0} \]
📌 Corollary 1.1: If \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive and \(P(A) > 0\), then \(P(B \mid A) = 0\). This shows that knowing \(A\) occurred makes \(B\) impossible.

📌 Corollary 1.2: For mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities, \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) > P(A)\). However, \(P(A \mid B) = 0 \neq P(A)\), confirming dependence.

📌 Corollary 1.3: The only way mutually exclusive events can be independent is if at least one of them has probability zero.
🟢

5. Theorem 2: Independent Events (with Positive Probabilities) Cannot be Mutually Exclusive

📌 Statement

If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent events and \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\), then they cannot be mutually exclusive (disjoint). In other words, two independent events (both possible) must have non-empty intersection.

📝 Proof:

Step 1 — State the given conditions:

Given that \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, we have:

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B) \]

Given: \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\). Therefore:

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) > 0 \]
Step 2 — If events were mutually exclusive, what would we expect?

If \(A\) and \(B\) were mutually exclusive, then:

\[ A \cap B = \emptyset \quad \Rightarrow \quad P(A \cap B) = 0 \]
Step 3 — Compare the two expressions:

From Step 1: \(P(A \cap B) > 0\). From Step 2: if mutually exclusive, \(P(A \cap B) = 0\).

Step 4 — Contradiction:

We have \(P(A \cap B) > 0\) and simultaneously would need \(P(A \cap B) = 0\). This is impossible.

Step 5 — Conclusion:

Therefore, independent events with positive probabilities cannot be mutually exclusive.

\[ \boxed{P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) > 0 \implies A \cap B \neq \emptyset} \]
📌 Corollary 2.1: If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent and \(P(A) > 0\), \(P(B) > 0\), then \(P(A \cap B) > 0\). This means the events have a non-empty overlap.

📌 Corollary 2.2: For independent events with positive probabilities, \(P(A \mid B) = P(A) > 0\), showing that the occurrence of B does not make A impossible.

📌 Corollary 2.3: The only way independent events can be mutually exclusive is if at least one of them has probability zero (impossible or almost impossible events).

7. Special Cases — When Can They Coincide?

📌 When can events be both mutually exclusive and independent?

For events \(A\) and \(B\) to be both mutually exclusive and independent, we must have:

\[ P(A \cap B) = 0 \quad \text{(mutually exclusive)} \]
\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) \quad \text{(independent)} \]

Therefore: \(P(A)P(B) = 0\)

This implies that at least one of \(P(A)\) or \(P(B)\) must be zero.

📌 Conclusion: Events can be both mutually exclusive and independent only when at least one event has probability zero. In practice, this means impossible events (or events that occur "almost never" in continuous spaces).

Example: Let \(A\) = "rolling a 7 on a standard die" (impossible, \(P(A)=0\)), and \(B\) = "rolling a 6". Then \(A\) and \(B\) are both mutually exclusive and independent (since \(P(A \cap B) = 0\) and \(P(A)P(B) = 0\)).
📝

8. Worked Examples

Example 1: Mutually Exclusive (Dependent)
A card is drawn from a standard deck. Let \(A\) = "card is a heart", \(B\) = "card is a spade". Are \(A\) and \(B\) mutually exclusive? Independent?

Solution:
\(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive because a card cannot be both a heart and a spade. \(P(A) = \frac{13}{52} = 0.25\), \(P(B) = 0.25\), \(P(A \cap B) = 0\).
\(P(A)P(B) = 0.0625 \neq 0\), so they are NOT independent — they are dependent.
Example 2: Independent (Not Mutually Exclusive)
A fair coin is tossed twice. Let \(A\) = "first toss is heads", \(B\) = "second toss is heads". Are \(A\) and \(B\) independent? Mutually exclusive?

Solution:
\(P(A) = 0.5\), \(P(B) = 0.5\), \(P(A \cap B) = 0.25\).
\(P(A)P(B) = 0.25 = P(A \cap B)\), so they are independent. Since \(P(A \cap B) = 0.25 > 0\), they are NOT mutually exclusive.
Thus, independent events (with positive probability) always have non-empty intersection.
Example 3: Neither Mutually Exclusive Nor Independent
Two cards are drawn without replacement. Let \(A\) = "first card is a king", \(B\) = "second card is a king". Are they mutually exclusive? Independent?

Solution:
\(P(A) = \frac{4}{52}\), \(P(B) = \frac{4}{52}\) (by symmetry). \(P(A \cap B) = \frac{4}{52} \cdot \frac{3}{51} \approx 0.00452\).
Since \(P(A \cap B) > 0\), they are NOT mutually exclusive.
\(P(A)P(B) \approx 0.00592 \neq 0.00452\), so they are NOT independent — they are dependent.
Thus, events can be both non-mutually-exclusive AND dependent.

9. Practice Problems

Problem 1: If \(P(A) = 0.3\), \(P(B) = 0.4\), and \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, find \(P(A \cup B)\) and \(P(A \cap B)\).

Show Solution

\(P(A \cap B) = 0\), \(P(A \cup B) = 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7\). They are dependent (since \(0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.12 \neq 0\)).

Problem 2: If \(P(A) = 0.5\), \(P(B) = 0.6\), and \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, find \(P(A \cap B)\) and \(P(A \cup B)\).

Show Solution

\(P(A \cap B) = 0.5 \times 0.6 = 0.3\), \(P(A \cup B) = 0.5 + 0.6 - 0.3 = 0.8\). Since \(P(A \cap B) > 0\), they are NOT mutually exclusive.

Problem 3: Can two events be both mutually exclusive and independent? If yes, under what condition?

Show Solution

Yes, when at least one event has probability zero. For example, \(A\) = impossible event (\(P(A)=0\)), \(B\) any event. Then \(P(A)P(B) = 0 = P(A \cap B)\).

Problem 4: Prove that if \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive and \(P(A) > 0\), then \(P(A \mid B) = 0\).

Show Solution

Since \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, \(A \cap B = \emptyset\), so \(P(A \cap B) = 0\). Then \(P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{0}{P(B)} = 0\).

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint): \(A \cap B = \emptyset \Rightarrow P(A \cap B) = 0\)
  • Independent: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\)
  • Theorem 1: Mutually exclusive events with \(P(A) > 0, P(B) > 0\) are always dependent.
  • Theorem 2: Independent events with \(P(A) > 0, P(B) > 0\) are never mutually exclusive.
  • Special Case: Events can be both only when at least one has probability zero.
  • Critical Distinction: Mutually exclusive = "cannot happen together"; Independent = "no influence on each other's probability".
