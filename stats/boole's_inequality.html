📊 Boole's Inequality (Union Bound)

The probability of a union is at most the sum of individual probabilities — and its dual for intersections

Probability Theory · Unit II · Boole's Inequality
📜

1. Introduction

Boole's Inequality (also called the Union Bound) is a fundamental result in probability theory that provides an upper bound on the probability of a union of events. It states that the probability of the union of any collection of events is at most the sum of their individual probabilities.

Named after the mathematician George Boole (1815-1864), this inequality is extremely useful when exact probabilities of intersections are difficult or impossible to compute. It appears in many areas including:

  • Concentration inequalities (e.g., Chernoff bounds, Markov's inequality)
  • Borel-Cantelli lemmas
  • Analysis of algorithms and randomized computations
  • Statistical learning theory and generalization bounds
➕

2. Boole's Inequality for Two Events

📌 Theorem (Boole's Inequality for Two Events — Union Form)

\[ P(A \cup B) \leq P(A) + P(B) \]

The probability that at least one of A or B occurs is at most the sum of their individual probabilities.

📝 Proof of Boole's Inequality for Two Events:

Step 1: From Kolmogorov's Axiom 1 (Non-negativity), we know that for any event, its probability is non-negative. In particular:
\[ P(A \cap B) \geq 0 \]
Step 2: Recall the addition theorem for two events:
\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

This is derived directly from Kolmogorov's axioms.

Step 3: Since \( P(A \cap B) \geq 0 \), we have:
\[ P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \leq P(A) + P(B) \]

Because subtracting a non-negative number (or zero) from \( P(A) + P(B) \) cannot increase the sum.

Step 4: Substitute the left-hand side using the addition theorem:
\[ P(A \cup B) \leq P(A) + P(B) \]
Step 5: This completes the proof. ∎
📌 Corollary 2.1: For any events A and B, \( P(A \cup B) \geq \max(P(A), P(B)) \).
📌 Corollary 2.2: If \( P(A) = 0 \), then \( P(A \cup B) \leq P(B) \), and since \( P(A \cup B) \geq P(B) \), we get \( P(A \cup B) = P(B) \).
📌 Corollary 2.3: The union bound is tight when A and B are mutually exclusive, because then \( P(A \cap B) = 0 \) and equality holds: \( P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \).

3. Boole's Inequality for n Events (Union Bound)

📌 Theorem (Boole's Inequality for n Events — Union Form)

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \]

The probability of the union of any finite collection of events is at most the sum of their individual probabilities.

📝 Proof of Boole's Inequality for n Events (by Mathematical Induction):

Step 1 — Base Case (n = 1):
\[ P(A_1) \leq P(A_1) \quad \text{✓ The inequality holds with equality.} \]
Step 2 — Base Case (n = 2):

We have already proved \( P(A_1 \cup A_2) \leq P(A_1) + P(A_2) \) from the previous proof. ✓ Base case holds.

Step 3 — Inductive Hypothesis:

Assume that Boole's inequality holds for any collection of \(m\) events, where \(m \geq 2\). That is:

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{m} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(A_i) \]
Step 4 — Inductive Step (prove for \(n = m+1\) events):

Consider \(m+1\) events \(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_m, A_{m+1}\). Define:

\[ B = \bigcup_{i=1}^{m} A_i \]

Then the union of all \(m+1\) events can be written as:

\[ \bigcup_{i=1}^{m+1} A_i = B \cup A_{m+1} \]
Step 5 — Apply Boole's inequality for two events to \(B\) and \(A_{m+1}\):
\[ P(B \cup A_{m+1}) \leq P(B) + P(A_{m+1}) \]
Step 6 — Apply the inductive hypothesis to \(P(B)\):

Since \(B = \bigcup_{i=1}^{m} A_i\), by the inductive hypothesis:

\[ P(B) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(A_i) \]
Step 7 — Combine the inequalities:
\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{m+1} A_i \right) = P(B \cup A_{m+1}) \leq P(B) + P(A_{m+1}) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(A_i) + P(A_{m+1}) = \sum_{i=1}^{m+1} P(A_i) \]
Step 8 — Conclusion:

Since the inequality holds for \(n = 1\) and \(n = 2\), and assuming it holds for \(n = m\), we have proved it holds for \(n = m+1\). Therefore, by the principle of mathematical induction, Boole's inequality holds for all positive integers \(n\):

\[ \boxed{P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i)} \]
📌 Corollary 3.1 (Countable Union Bound): For a countably infinite collection of events \(A_1, A_2, A_3, \ldots\):
\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i) \]
This follows from the finite case by taking the limit as \(n \to \infty\) and using the continuity of probability.

📌 Corollary 3.2 (Borel-Cantelli Lemma — First Part): If \(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i) < \infty\), then \(P(\limsup_{n \to \infty} A_n) = 0\). This is a direct consequence of countable Boole's inequality.

📌 Corollary 3.3 (Simple Bound for Complement): Since \(P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) \geq P(A_i)\) for any \(i\):
\[ \max_{1 \leq i \leq n} P(A_i) \leq P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \]
∩

4. Dual of Boole's Inequality (Lower Bound for Intersection)

📌 Theorem (Dual Boole Inequality — Lower Bound for Intersection)

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i^c) \]

Equivalently, for two events:

\[ P(A \cap B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1 \]

This gives a lower bound on the probability of the intersection of events.

📝 Proof of Dual Boole Inequality for Two Events:

Step 1: Start with Boole's inequality for two events applied to the complements:
\[ P(A^c \cup B^c) \leq P(A^c) + P(B^c) \]
Step 2: By De Morgan's law, \( A^c \cup B^c = (A \cap B)^c \). Therefore:
\[ P((A \cap B)^c) \leq P(A^c) + P(B^c) \]
Step 3: Apply the complement rule: \( P((A \cap B)^c) = 1 - P(A \cap B) \).
\[ 1 - P(A \cap B) \leq P(A^c) + P(B^c) \]
Step 4: Rearranging the inequality:
\[ 1 - P(A \cap B) \leq (1 - P(A)) + (1 - P(B)) \]
\[ 1 - P(A \cap B) \leq 2 - P(A) - P(B) \]
Step 5: Subtract 1 from both sides and multiply by -1 (reversing the inequality):
\[ -P(A \cap B) \leq 1 - P(A) - P(B) \]
\[ P(A \cap B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1 \]
Step 6: This completes the proof. ∎

📝 Alternative Proof of Dual Inequality (using union bound directly):

Step 1: Note that \( A \cap B = (A^c \cup B^c)^c \) by De Morgan's law.
Step 2: Apply the complement rule:
\[ P(A \cap B) = 1 - P(A^c \cup B^c) \]
Step 3: Apply Boole's inequality to \( P(A^c \cup B^c) \):
\[ P(A^c \cup B^c) \leq P(A^c) + P(B^c) \]
Step 4: Therefore:
\[ P(A \cap B) \geq 1 - [P(A^c) + P(B^c)] \]
\[ P(A \cap B) \geq 1 - [(1 - P(A)) + (1 - P(B))] \]
\[ P(A \cap B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1 \]

📌 Dual Boole Inequality for n Events (General Form)

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i^c) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} (1 - P(A_i)) \]

Equivalently:

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - (n - 1) \]

📝 Proof of Dual Boole Inequality for n Events (by Induction):

Step 1 — Base Case (n = 1):
\[ P(A_1) \geq P(A_1) \quad \text{(trivially true)} \]
Step 2 — Base Case (n = 2):

We have already proved \( P(A_1 \cap A_2) \geq P(A_1) + P(A_2) - 1 \). ✓

Step 3 — Inductive Hypothesis:

Assume that the dual inequality holds for any collection of \(m\) events, where \(m \geq 2\):

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(A_i) - (m - 1) \]
Step 4 — Inductive Step (prove for \(n = m+1\) events):

Consider \(m+1\) events. We can write:

\[ \bigcap_{i=1}^{m+1} A_i = \left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} A_i \right) \cap A_{m+1} \]
Step 5 — Apply the dual inequality for two events:
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m+1} A_i \right) \geq P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} A_i \right) + P(A_{m+1}) - 1 \]
Step 6 — Apply the inductive hypothesis to \( P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} A_i \right) \):
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(A_i) - (m - 1) \]
Step 7 — Combine the inequalities:
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m+1} A_i \right) \geq \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(A_i) - (m - 1) \right] + P(A_{m+1}) - 1 \]
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m+1} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{m+1} P(A_i) - m \]
Step 8 — Conclusion:

Therefore, by induction, for all \(n \geq 1\):

\[ \boxed{P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i^c)} \]
📌 Corollary 4.1 (Bonferroni's First Inequality): For any collection of n events:
\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} P(A_i \cap A_j) \]
This is the first Bonferroni inequality, which is stronger than the simple union bound.

📌 Corollary 4.2 (Lower Bound for Intersection): If each event \(A_i\) has probability at least \(p\), then:
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq np - (n - 1) \]
📌 Corollary 4.3: If \( \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) > n - 1 \), then \( P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) > 0 \). This gives a sufficient condition for a non-empty intersection with positive probability.

5. Summary of Boole's Inequality and Its Dual

📌 Union Form (Boole's Inequality):

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \]

Interpretation: The probability that at least one event occurs is at most the sum of individual probabilities.

📌 Intersection Form (Dual Boole Inequality):

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i^c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - (n - 1) \]

Interpretation: The probability that all events occur is at least the sum of individual probabilities minus (n-1).

📝

6. Worked Examples

📖 Example 1: Using Union Bound

A system has 10 components, each with failure probability 0.05. The system fails if any component fails. Find an upper bound on the probability of system failure.

Solution:
Let \(A_i\) be the event that component \(i\) fails. Then system failure is \( \bigcup_{i=1}^{10} A_i \).
By Boole's inequality:
\[ P(\text{system failure}) = P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{10} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{10} P(A_i) = 10 \times 0.05 = 0.5 \]
So the probability of system failure is at most 50%.
📖 Example 2: Using Dual Inequality

Three events have probabilities \(P(A_1) = 0.9\), \(P(A_2) = 0.8\), \(P(A_3) = 0.7\). Find a lower bound for \(P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3)\).

Solution:
By the dual Boole inequality for three events:
\[ P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3) \geq P(A_1) + P(A_2) + P(A_3) - 2 = 0.9 + 0.8 + 0.7 - 2 = 2.4 - 2 = 0.4 \]
So the probability that all three events occur is at least 40%.
📖 Example 3: Application to Borel-Cantelli

Let \(A_1, A_2, A_3, \ldots\) be events such that \( \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i) < \infty \). Show that \(P(\limsup A_n) = 0\).

Solution Outline:
Define \(B_n = \bigcup_{k=n}^{\infty} A_k\). Then \(\limsup A_n = \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\).
By Boole's inequality, \(P(B_n) \leq \sum_{k=n}^{\infty} P(A_k)\).
Since the tail sum goes to 0 as \(n \to \infty\), \(P(B_n) \to 0\). Then \(P(\limsup A_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(B_n) = 0\).

7. Practice Problems

Problem 1: If \(P(A) = 0.6\) and \(P(B) = 0.5\), what is the range of possible values for \(P(A \cup B)\)?

Show Solution

By Boole's inequality: \(P(A \cup B) \leq 0.6 + 0.5 = 1.1\), but probability cannot exceed 1, so \(P(A \cup B) \leq 1\). Also \(P(A \cup B) \geq \max(0.6, 0.5) = 0.6\). Therefore \(0.6 \leq P(A \cup B) \leq 1\).

Problem 2: For four events with \(P(A_i) = 0.3\) for all i, what is the minimum possible value of \(P(\bigcap_{i=1}^{4} A_i)\)?

Show Solution

By dual Boole inequality: \(P(\bigcap_{i=1}^{4} A_i) \geq 0.3 + 0.3 + 0.3 + 0.3 - 3 = 1.2 - 3 = -1.8\). Since probability cannot be negative, the inequality gives no useful lower bound (it's trivially true). The actual minimum is 0, which can occur if the events are mutually exclusive in a certain way.

Problem 3: Prove that \(P(A \cap B) \geq 1 - P(A^c) - P(B^c)\) using Boole's inequality.

Show Solution

By Boole: \(P(A^c \cup B^c) \leq P(A^c) + P(B^c)\). By De Morgan: \(P((A \cap B)^c) \leq P(A^c) + P(B^c)\). Then \(1 - P(A \cap B) \leq P(A^c) + P(B^c)\). Rearranging: \(P(A \cap B) \geq 1 - P(A^c) - P(B^c)\).

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Boole's Inequality (Union Bound): \(P(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i)\)
  • Proof: Uses the addition theorem \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\) and the fact that \(P(A \cap B) \geq 0\), extended to n events by induction
  • Dual Boole Inequality (Intersection Bound): \(P(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - (n - 1)\)
  • Derivation: From Boole's inequality applied to complements and De Morgan's law
  • Applications: Borel-Cantelli lemmas, concentration inequalities, reliability theory
  • Both forms are tight: Equality when events are mutually exclusive (for union) or when events are nested (for intersection)
