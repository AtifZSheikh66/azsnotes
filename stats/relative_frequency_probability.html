📊 Statistical (Empirical) Probability

The relative frequency interpretation — probability from observed data

Probability Theory · Unit I · Empirical Definition
📜

1. Defining Empirical Probability

Statistical (or Empirical) Probability is defined as the relative frequency of an event occurring when an experiment is repeated a large number of times. It is based on observed data rather than theoretical assumptions.

Unlike classical probability, which assumes equally likely outcomes a priori, empirical probability is derived directly from experimentation or observation. The empirical probability of an event \(E\) is calculated as:

📌 Empirical Probability Formula

\[ P_n(E) = \frac{\text{Number of times } E \text{ occurs in } n \text{ trials}}{n} = \frac{f_n(E)}{n} \]
Where:
  • \(n\) is the total number of trials or observations
  • \(f_n(E)\) is the frequency (count) of event \(E\) occurring in \(n\) trials
  • \(P_n(E)\) is the empirical probability based on \(n\) trials

Key Insight: Empirical probability estimates the true (theoretical) probability by observing actual outcomes. As the number of trials increases, the empirical probability tends to stabilize around the true probability — this is the Law of Large Numbers.

📈

2. The Relative Frequency Interpretation

The relative frequency interpretation of probability is one of the major interpretations of probability alongside classical and subjective interpretations[citation:1]. According to this view, the probability of an event is the long-run proportion of times the event occurs when the experiment is repeated infinitely many times.

📌 Relative Frequency as Probability Limit

\[ P(E) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f_n(E)}{n} \]
The true probability \(P(E)\) is the limit of the relative frequency as the number of trials approaches infinity.
📖 Example: Rolling a Die
Suppose we roll a die 1000 times and observe the number 6 appearing 163 times. The empirical probability of rolling a 6 is:
\[ P_{1000}(6) = \frac{163}{1000} = 0.163 \]
If we continued rolling indefinitely, the relative frequency would approach the theoretical probability \( \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.1667 \)[citation:8]. The more trials we conduct, the closer we get to the true value.
💡 Terminology Note: "Statistical probability," "empirical probability," and "relative frequency probability" are used interchangeably. All refer to probabilities derived from observed data rather than theoretical assumptions[citation:4].
🔬

3. Calculating Empirical Probability

To calculate empirical probability, follow these steps:

  1. Define the experiment and the event of interest
  2. Conduct \(n\) trials (observations) of the experiment
  3. Record the frequency of the event occurring
  4. Calculate the ratio \( \frac{\text{frequency}}{n} \)
📖 Example: Carnival Game
A carnival game requires contestants to throw a softball at a stack of three bottles. The goal is to knock over all three bottles. We want to find the probability that a randomly selected contestant wins.

Since the outcomes are not equally likely (skill, throwing angle, etc. vary), we cannot use classical probability. Instead, we observe the game over a weekend: 582 games played, 32 winners.[citation:4]
\[ P(\text{win}) = \frac{32}{582} \approx 0.055 \text{ or } 5.5\% \]
📖 Example: Bottled Water Study
A study of bottled water revealed that among samples tested, 40% were merely tap water and 30% were contaminated by pollutants[citation:4].
Let \(E\) = "A randomly selected bottled water sample is actually tap water"
Let \(F\) = "A randomly selected bottled water sample is contaminated"
Then based on the study data:
\[ P(E) = 0.40 \quad \text{and} \quad P(F) = 0.30 \]

5. The Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers (LLN) states that as the number of independent trials increases, the empirical probability (relative frequency) of an event converges to its true theoretical probability[citation:2][citation:6].

📌 Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P\left( \left| \frac{S_n}{n} - p \right| > \varepsilon \right) = 0 \quad \text{for any } \varepsilon > 0 \]
Where \(S_n\) is the number of successes in \(n\) trials and \(p\) is the true probability of success. This means that for large \(n\), the empirical probability is close to \(p\) with high probability[citation:3].

Key Implications of the Law of Large Numbers

  • Empirical probabilities become more reliable as sample size increases
  • It justifies using relative frequencies as estimates of true probabilities
  • It explains why insurance companies can predict losses accurately despite individual uncertainty
  • It does NOT imply that past outcomes affect future outcomes (the gambler's fallacy)

Important Clarification — Gambler's Fallacy: The Law of Large Numbers does NOT say that if an event hasn't occurred as often as expected, it is "due" to occur. Being "due" suggests the probability has increased for later trials, which is incorrect. The law describes long-term convergence, not short-term compensation[citation:2].

⚖️

6. Weak Law vs. Strong Law of Large Numbers

Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)

Convergence "in probability": The probability that the empirical average deviates from the true value becomes arbitrarily small as \(n\) increases.

\[ \frac{S_n}{n} \xrightarrow{P} p \]

Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)

Convergence "almost surely": The empirical average converges to the true value with probability 1.

\[ \frac{S_n}{n} \xrightarrow{a.s.} p \]
💡 Practical Difference: The Weak Law tells us that for any given margin of error, large enough samples will make large deviations rare. The Strong Law tells us that with probability 1, the sample average will eventually stay arbitrarily close to the true value and never deviate again. Both support the use of empirical probabilities[citation:3].
⚔️

7. Comparison: Empirical vs. Classical Probability

📐 Classical (A Priori) Probability

  • Based on deductive reasoning
  • Assumes equally likely outcomes
  • No experimentation required
  • Example: Fair coin → \(P(H) = 0.5\)
  • Limited to symmetric situations

📊 Empirical (A Posteriori) Probability

  • Based on observed data
  • No assumption of equal likelihood
  • Requires repeated experimentation
  • Example: Observed 32 wins in 582 games → \(P \approx 0.055\)
  • Applicable to any repeatable experiment[citation:4]
📖 When to Use Each Approach:

Classical: Rolling a fair die, drawing from a shuffled deck, tossing a fair coin — situations where physical symmetry ensures equally likely outcomes.

Empirical: Estimating the probability that a randomly selected person has a certain disease, the probability a machine fails, the probability a student passes an exam — situations where equally likely outcomes cannot be assumed[citation:4].

8. Properties of Empirical Probability

Empirical probabilities satisfy the same fundamental properties as classical probabilities:

\[ 0 \leq P_n(E) \leq 1 \] \[ P_n(S) = 1 \quad \text{(the event always occurs in all trials)} \] \[ P_n(\emptyset) = 0 \quad \text{(the impossible event never occurs)} \]

Additivity Property

For mutually exclusive events \(A\) and \(B\) (they cannot occur simultaneously):

\[ P_n(A \cup B) = P_n(A) + P_n(B) \]

This follows directly from counting: the frequency of \(A \cup B\) is the sum of individual frequencies when \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive[citation:2].

9. Real-World Applications

📖 Insurance and Risk Assessment
Insurance companies use historical data to estimate the probability of claims. For example, if 5,000 out of 100,000 insured drivers had accidents in a year, the empirical probability of a claim is \( \frac{5000}{100000} = 0.05 \). This 5% rate is used to set premiums.
📖 Quality Control
A factory tests a sample of 1,000 light bulbs and finds 15 defective. The empirical probability of a defective bulb is \( \frac{15}{1000} = 0.015 \) or 1.5%. This estimate guides quality assurance decisions.
📖 Medical Studies
In a clinical trial of 500 patients, 350 show improvement after treatment. The empirical probability of improvement is \( \frac{350}{500} = 0.70 \) or 70%. This empirical evidence supports the treatment's effectiveness.

10. Worked Examples

Example 1: Dice Roll Experiment
A die is rolled 60 times. The results are:
Face value: 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6
Frequency: 8 | 10 | 9 | 11 | 12 | 10

Find the empirical probability of:
(a) Rolling a 5 (b) Rolling an even number (c) Rolling a number greater than 4

Solution:
Total rolls: \(n = 60\)
(a) \(P(5) = \frac{12}{60} = 0.2\)
(b) Even numbers: 2, 4, 6 → frequencies: 10 + 11 + 10 = 31 → \(P = \frac{31}{60} \approx 0.517\)
(c) Numbers > 4: 5, 6 → frequencies: 12 + 10 = 22 → \(P = \frac{22}{60} = \frac{11}{30} \approx 0.367\)
Example 2: Cowboy Study
According to a medical study, 63% of cowboys suffer from saddle sores, 52% suffer from bowed legs, and 40% suffer from both conditions[citation:4]. Let \(E\) = "cowboy has saddle sores," \(F\) = "cowboy has bowed legs."
Find: (a) \(P(E)\) (b) \(P(F)\) (c) \(P(E \cap F)\) (d) \(P(E \cup F)\)

Solution:
(a) \(P(E) = 0.63\) (b) \(P(F) = 0.52\) (c) \(P(E \cap F) = 0.40\)
(d) Using addition rule: \(P(E \cup F) = 0.63 + 0.52 - 0.40 = 0.75\)

11. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1 — The Gambler's Fallacy: Believing that past outcomes affect future independent trials. For example, after a long streak of heads, thinking tails is "due" to occur. This is false; each trial is independent with the same probability[citation:2].

Misconception 2 — Small Sample Reliability: Believing that empirical probabilities from small samples are reliable estimates. In reality, small samples can produce highly variable results; the Law of Large Numbers requires many trials to achieve stability[citation:8].

Misconception 3 — Exactness: Thinking empirical probability exactly equals the theoretical probability. Empirical probability is an estimate that converges to the true value but rarely equals it exactly for finite \(n\).

12. Practice Problems

Problem 1: A coin is tossed 200 times and lands heads 112 times. What is the empirical probability of heads? Based on this, would you suspect the coin is fair?

Show Solution

\(P(H) = \frac{112}{200} = 0.56\). A fair coin would give 0.50. While 0.56 is different, with only 200 trials this difference could be due to chance. More trials would be needed to determine if the coin is truly biased.

Problem 2: Over three years, a factory produced 50,000 widgets. Of these, 425 were defective. What is the empirical probability that a randomly selected widget is defective?

Show Solution

\(P(\text{defective}) = \frac{425}{50000} = 0.0085 = 0.85\%\)

Problem 3: A survey of 1,000 households found that 320 had a pet dog, 280 had a pet cat, and 120 had both. What is the empirical probability that a household has a dog or a cat?

Show Solution

\(P(D) = 0.32\), \(P(C) = 0.28\), \(P(D \cap C) = 0.12\)
\(P(D \cup C) = 0.32 + 0.28 - 0.12 = 0.48\)

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Empirical (Statistical) Probability: \(P_n(E) = \frac{\text{frequency of } E}{n}\) — based on observed data
  • The Law of Large Numbers (LLN): As \(n \to \infty\), the empirical probability converges to the true theoretical probability
  • Empirical probability is essential when equally likely outcomes cannot be assumed
  • The Weak Law deals with convergence in probability; the Strong Law with almost sure convergence
  • Common applications include quality control, insurance, medicine, and social sciences
  • Important: Empirical probability estimates improve with more trials; small samples may be unreliable
