📉 Bonferroni's First Inequality

A refinement of Boole's inequality — lower bound for the union probability

Probability Theory · Unit II · Bonferroni Inequalities
📜

1. Introduction

Bonferroni's First Inequality (also called the first Bonferroni inequality) provides a lower bound for the probability of the union of events. While Boole's inequality gives an upper bound (\(P(A \cup B) \leq P(A) + P(B)\)), Bonferroni's first inequality gives a lower bound that takes into account pairwise intersections.

Named after the Italian mathematician Carlo Emilio Bonferroni (1892-1960), these inequalities are fundamental in multiple hypothesis testing, simultaneous inference, and probability theory.

➕

2. Bonferroni's First Inequality for Two Events

📌 Theorem (Bonferroni's First Inequality — Two Events)

\[ P(A \cup B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

Wait — this is the addition theorem itself! Actually, the addition theorem is an equality, not an inequality. Bonferroni's first inequality is typically expressed as a lower bound for the union in terms of individual probabilities and pairwise intersections. But the addition theorem itself gives the exact value:

\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

From this, since \(P(A \cap B) \geq 0\), we get:

\[ P(A \cup B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1 \]

Also, since \(P(A \cap B) \leq \min(P(A), P(B))\), we get:

\[ P(A \cup B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - \min(P(A), P(B)) = \max(P(A), P(B)) \]

📝 Derivation of Lower Bound for Two Events:

Step 1: From the addition theorem (which follows directly from Axiom 3):
\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]
Step 2: Since \(P(A \cap B) \leq 1\) (by Axiom 1, probabilities are ≤ 1), we have:
\[ -P(A \cap B) \geq -1 \]
Step 3: Adding \(P(A) + P(B)\) to both sides:
\[ P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1 \]
Step 4: Therefore:
\[ P(A \cup B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1 \]
Step 5 — Alternative derivation using the dual of Boole's inequality:

From the dual Boole inequality: \(P(A \cap B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1\).

But this is different — it gives a lower bound for the intersection, not the union.

📌 Corollary 2.1: For any two events A and B, \(P(A \cup B) \geq \max(P(A), P(B))\).

📌 Corollary 2.2: \(P(A \cup B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1\). This is the simplest form of Bonferroni's first inequality.

📌 Corollary 2.3: If \(P(A) + P(B) > 1\), then \(P(A \cap B) > 0\) (since \(P(A \cap B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1 > 0\)).

3. Bonferroni's First Inequality for n Events

📌 Theorem (Bonferroni's First Inequality — n Events)

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} P(A_i \cap A_j) \]

This gives a lower bound on the probability of the union in terms of individual probabilities and pairwise intersections.

Equivalently:

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{i < j} P(A_i \cap A_j) \]

📝 Proof of Bonferroni's First Inequality for n Events:

Step 1 — Recall the inclusion-exclusion principle (exact formula):
\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \sum_{1 \leq i < j < k \leq n} P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k) - \cdots + (-1)^{n-1} P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \]
Step 2 — Observe the alternating sum structure:

The inclusion-exclusion formula alternates signs: +, −, +, −, …

Step 3 — Consider the sum of triple and higher-order intersections:
\[ S_3 = \sum_{1 \leq i < j < k \leq n} P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k) \]
\[ S_4 = \sum_{1 \leq i < j < k < l \leq n} P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k \cap A_l) \]

and so on for higher-order intersections.

Step 4 — Group the terms after pairwise intersections:
\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) = \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{i
Step 5 — Show that the bracketed term is non-negative:

For any fixed set of indices, consider the principle of inclusion-exclusion applied to just three events:

\[ P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) - P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C) \]

Since the left-hand side is a probability, it is ≥ 0. Therefore:

\[ P(A \cap B \cap C) \geq -[P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) - P(B \cap C)] \]

More directly, from the inclusion-exclusion principle, we have the following general inequality: the partial sums alternate as upper and lower bounds. In particular:

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{i

This is because the next term (sum over triple intersections) is added with a positive sign, so removing it decreases the right-hand side, giving a lower bound.

Step 6 — Formal justification using Bonferroni's inequalities (by induction):

Bonferroni proved that the partial sums of the inclusion-exclusion series alternately give upper and lower bounds. Specifically:

  • The first sum (single events) gives an upper bound: \(P(\bigcup A_i) \leq \sum P(A_i)\) — this is Boole's inequality.
  • The sum up to pairwise intersections gives a lower bound: \(P(\bigcup A_i) \geq \sum P(A_i) - \sum_{i
  • The sum up to triple intersections gives an upper bound, and so on.
Step 7 — Alternative inductive proof:

Base case (n = 2): From the addition theorem:

\[ P(A_1 \cup A_2) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) - P(A_1 \cap A_2) \geq P(A_1) + P(A_2) - P(A_1 \cap A_2) \]

Inductive step: Assume the inequality holds for n events. For n+1 events:

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n+1} A_i \right) = P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) + P(A_{n+1}) - P\left( \left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \cap A_{n+1} \right) \]

By the distributive law: \(\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \cap A_{n+1} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} (A_i \cap A_{n+1})\)

Applying Boole's inequality to this union:

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} (A_i \cap A_{n+1}) \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i \cap A_{n+1}) \]

Therefore:

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n+1} A_i \right) \geq P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) + P(A_{n+1}) - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i \cap A_{n+1}) \]

Applying the inductive hypothesis to \(P(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i)\):

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n+1} A_i \right) \geq \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} P(A_i \cap A_j) \right] + P(A_{n+1}) - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i \cap A_{n+1}) \]
\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n+1} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} P(A_i) - \left[ \sum_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i \cap A_{n+1}) \right] \]
\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n+1} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} P(A_i) - \sum_{1 \leq i < j \leq n+1} P(A_i \cap A_j) \]

Thus, by induction, the inequality holds for all n. ∎

📌 Corollary 3.1 (Bonferroni's Second Inequality): The partial sum including triple intersections gives an upper bound:
\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{i 📌 Corollary 3.2 (Simplified Form): A weaker but often useful form:
\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{i 📌 Corollary 3.3 (Multiple Testing Context): In hypothesis testing with m tests, if each individual test has significance level α, the probability of at least one false rejection is at most mα by Boole, but Bonferroni gives: \(P(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m} \text{Reject}_i) \leq m\alpha\). The complementary (lower bound) form is also useful.

📌 Corollary 3.4: For n = 3, Bonferroni's first inequality becomes:
\[ P(A \cup B \cup C) \geq P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) - P(B \cap C) \]

4. Bonferroni vs. Boole: A Comparison

📌 Boole's Inequality

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \]

Provides an upper bound. Uses only individual probabilities.

📌 Bonferroni's First Inequality

\[ P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{i

Provides a lower bound. Uses individual probabilities AND pairwise intersections.

Together, Boole's inequality and Bonferroni's first inequality give two-sided bounds:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \sum_{i

For n = 2, this becomes:

\[ P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \leq P(A \cup B) \leq P(A) + P(B) \]

The left inequality is actually an equality (addition theorem), so it's tight.

5. Alternative Forms

📌 Form 1 — Complement Form

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i^c) - \sum_{i

This follows by applying Bonferroni's first inequality to the complements and using De Morgan's law.

📌 Form 2 — Probability of Intersection Lower Bound (Bonferroni's Intersection Bound)

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - (n - 1) \]

This is actually the dual Bonferroni inequality (also called the first Bonferroni inequality in some texts), which we derived earlier from Boole's inequality.

📝 Derivation of the Intersection Form:

Step 1: Start with the dual Boole inequality:
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i^c) \]
Step 2: Substitute \(P(A_i^c) = 1 - P(A_i)\):
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} (1 - P(A_i)) \]
Step 3: Simplify:
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq 1 - \left[ n - \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \right] \]
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - (n - 1) \]
📌 Corollary 5.1: If \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) > n - 1\), then \(P(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i) > 0\).

📌 Corollary 5.2: For two events, this gives: \(P(A \cap B) \geq P(A) + P(B) - 1\).

📌 Corollary 5.3: For equal probabilities \(P(A_i) = p\), the intersection bound becomes: \(P(\bigcap A_i) \geq np - (n - 1)\).

6. Worked Examples

📖 Example 1: Using Bonferroni's First Inequality

Three events have probabilities: \(P(A_1)=0.7\), \(P(A_2)=0.6\), \(P(A_3)=0.5\). Their pairwise intersections are: \(P(A_1 \cap A_2)=0.4\), \(P(A_1 \cap A_3)=0.3\), \(P(A_2 \cap A_3)=0.2\). Bound \(P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3)\) from below using Bonferroni's first inequality.

Solution:
By Bonferroni's first inequality:
\[ P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3) \geq P(A_1)+P(A_2)+P(A_3) - [P(A_1 \cap A_2)+P(A_1 \cap A_3)+P(A_2 \cap A_3)] \]
\[ = 0.7 + 0.6 + 0.5 - (0.4 + 0.3 + 0.2) = 1.8 - 0.9 = 0.9 \]
So \(P(\bigcup A_i) \geq 0.9\). For comparison, Boole gives \(P(\bigcup A_i) \leq 1.8\), which is trivial (>1). The exact value (by inclusion-exclusion) would require the triple intersection.
📖 Example 2: Lower Bound for Complement of Union

Suppose \(P(A_i) = 0.1\) for 100 events, and pairwise intersections are at most 0.01. Find a lower bound for the probability that none of the events occur.

Solution:
Let \(E = \bigcup_{i=1}^{100} A_i\). We want \(P(E^c) = 1 - P(E)\).
By Bonferroni's first inequality:
\[ P(E) \geq \sum_{i=1}^{100} P(A_i) - \sum_{i
\[ P(E) \geq 100 \times 0.1 - \binom{100}{2} \times 0.01 \]
\[ \binom{100}{2} = 4950 \]
\[ P(E) \geq 10 - 4950 \times 0.01 = 10 - 49.5 = -39.5 \]

Since probability cannot be negative, this lower bound is trivial (0). We need a better bound. Using the weaker dual Bonferroni:

\[ P(E^c) \geq 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{100} P(A_i) = 1 - 10 = -9 \text{ (trivial)} \]

Actually, with these numbers, the upper bound from Boole is \(P(E) \leq 100 \times 0.1 = 10\) (trivial). The exact calculation would require more precise knowledge of intersections.

📖 Example 3: Applying Bonferroni to Multiple Testing

In a multiple hypothesis testing scenario, 10 independent tests are each conducted at significance level α = 0.05. Find an upper bound on the family-wise error rate (FWER) using Boole, and a lower bound on the probability of no false rejections.

Solution:
Let \(A_i\) be the event of a Type I error (false rejection) for test i. By Boole:
\[ FWER = P(\bigcup_{i=1}^{10} A_i) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{10} P(A_i) = 10 \times 0.05 = 0.5 \]

For the probability of no false rejections (all nulls retained), we have:

\[ P(\text{no error}) = P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{10} A_i^c \right) \]

If the tests are independent:

\[ P(\text{no error}) = (1 - 0.05)^{10} = 0.95^{10} \approx 0.5987 \]

The Bonferroni lower bound (from dual form) would give:

\[ P(\bigcap A_i^c) \geq \sum P(A_i^c) - 9 = 10 \times 0.95 - 9 = 9.5 - 9 = 0.5 \]

Thus \(0.5 \leq P(\text{no error}) \leq 1\), which is consistent with the exact value 0.5987.

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Bonferroni's First Inequality: \(P(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i) \geq \sum_{i=1}^n P(A_i) - \sum_{i
  • Provides a lower bound for the probability of a union, complementing Boole's upper bound
  • Derived from the inclusion-exclusion principle by truncating after the pairwise intersection term and using the alternating property
  • For n = 2, it is an equality (the addition theorem itself)
  • Related Forms: The dual form \(P(\bigcap A_i) \geq \sum P(A_i) - (n-1)\) is often also called Bonferroni's inequality
  • Applications: Multiple hypothesis testing (family-wise error rate), bounding probabilities, and confidence regions
