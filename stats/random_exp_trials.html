🧪 Random Experiment & Trial

The fundamental building blocks of probability theory: understanding processes that generate uncertain outcomes

Probability Theory · Unit I · Core Concepts
🔬

1. Random Experiment

A random experiment is a process or procedure that produces an uncertain outcome, can be repeated any number of times under identical conditions, and has a well-defined set of all possible outcomes.

Essential Characteristics

  • Multiple possible outcomes — at least two different results can occur
  • Outcome uncertainty — impossible to predict the exact outcome before performing the experiment
  • Repeatable — can be performed again and again under similar conditions
  • Well-defined sample space — all possible outcomes are known in advance, even if the actual result is not
🎲 Real-World Random Experiments:

• Coin toss — outcomes: Heads or Tails
• Die roll — outcomes: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6
• Card draw — outcomes: any of the 52 cards in a deck
• Spinning a roulette wheel — outcomes: 0, 00, or 1-36
• Measuring daily rainfall — outcomes: any non-negative real number (in mm)
• Number of customers arriving at a store in an hour — outcomes: 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
⚖️

2. Random vs. Deterministic Experiments

🔧 Deterministic Experiment

An experiment that produces the same outcome every time under identical conditions. Only one possible outcome exists.

Examples:

  • Adding 2 + 3 — always yields 5
  • Boiling water at 100°C at sea level — always produces steam
  • Dropping a ball from a fixed height — always falls to the ground

🎲 Random Experiment

An experiment that can produce different outcomes even when conditions are identical. Multiple possible outcomes exist.

Examples:

  • Flipping a coin — could be Heads or Tails
  • Rolling a die — could be any number 1 through 6
  • Drawing a lottery ticket — varies each time
💡 Key Insight: The distinction lies in predictability. Deterministic experiments are fully predictable; random experiments are not. However, random experiments exhibit statistical regularity — the pattern of outcomes becomes predictable in the long run.
🔄

3. Trial

A trial (or repetition) is a single performance of a random experiment. It results in exactly one outcome from the sample space.

When we perform a random experiment multiple times, each individual performance is called a trial. The collection of all trials forms a sequence of repetitions.

📖 Understanding Trials:

• If you flip a coin 50 times, each flip is one trial.
• If you roll a die 100 times, each roll is a separate trial.
• If you draw 5 cards from a deck (without replacement), the entire draw of 5 cards can be considered a single trial of a more complex experiment, or each card draw could be a trial — context matters.
📌 Important: Trials of the same random experiment are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (unless stated otherwise). This means the outcome of one trial does not affect the outcome of another, and the probability of each outcome remains constant across trials.
🔍

4. Trial vs. Experiment: Clarifying the Terms

In probability literature, the terms "experiment" and "trial" are sometimes used interchangeably, but there is a subtle and important distinction, especially when discussing repeated experiments.

Aspect Experiment Trial
Definition The entire procedure or process under study A single execution or repetition of the experiment
Scope Broader — may consist of many repetitions Individual — one instance of the experiment
Analogy A clinical study (overall research) One patient's treatment and observation
Example "Coin-flipping experiment (100 flips)" "One coin flip"
Mathematical Usage Often refers to the underlying probability model Refers to each realization of the model
📊 Concrete Example:

Suppose a researcher wants to study the fairness of a coin.

• Experiment: Tossing the coin 500 times to collect data.
• Trial: Each individual toss of the coin.

The experiment consists of 500 trials. After completing all trials, the researcher can estimate the probability of heads.
🧩

5. Composed Experiment

A composed experiment is an experiment that consists of performing several simpler experiments (trials) in sequence. The overall outcome is an ordered sequence of the outcomes of each individual trial.

When we perform multiple trials of the same random experiment, we are actually conducting a composed experiment. The sample space of a composed experiment is the Cartesian product of the sample spaces of the individual trials.

📖 Examples of Composed Experiments:

• Two coin tosses: The composed experiment has sample space {HH, HT, TH, TT}. Each letter represents the outcome of one trial.
• Rolling a die three times: The sample space contains 6 × 6 × 6 = 216 ordered triples like (1,3,5).
• Drawing two cards from a deck (with replacement): The composed experiment allows any ordered pair of cards.
💡 Visualization: Tree diagrams are excellent tools for representing composed experiments. Each branch represents a trial outcome, and each path from root to leaf represents one complete outcome of the composed experiment.
📈

6. Statistical Regularity

Although individual trials are unpredictable, the pattern of outcomes across many trials exhibits statistical regularity. This is the fundamental principle that makes probability useful.

Key Principle

As the number of trials increases, the relative frequency of each outcome tends to stabilize around a fixed number — the probability of that outcome.

📊 Illustration with Coin Tosses:

• After 10 trials: 7 heads (70% heads) → unstable
• After 100 trials: 52 heads (52% heads) → closer to 50%
• After 1,000 trials: 498 heads (49.8% heads) → very close to 50%
• After 10,000 trials: 5,012 heads (50.12% heads) → extremely stable

The relative frequency converges to the true probability (0.5 for a fair coin).
📌 Why This Matters: Statistical regularity is the foundation of the frequency interpretation of probability. It allows us to estimate unknown probabilities by performing many trials and observing relative frequencies. This principle is formalized in the Law of Large Numbers.
📂

7. Classification of Random Experiments

Random experiments can be classified based on the nature of their sample space:

🔢 Finite

Sample space contains a finite (countable) number of outcomes.

Examples:

  • Coin toss (2 outcomes)
  • Die roll (6 outcomes)
  • Card draw (52 outcomes)

∞ Countably Infinite

Sample space is infinite but can be listed (countable).

Examples:

  • Number of coin tosses until first heads
  • Number of customers arriving (0,1,2,...)
  • Number of particles emitted by a radioactive source

📏 Uncountably Infinite

Sample space is infinite and cannot be listed (uncountable).

Examples:

  • Measuring temperature (any real number)
  • Waiting time for a bus (any non-negative real)
  • Angle of a spinning pointer (0 to 2π radians)
📋

8. Summary: Quick Reference

✅ Random Experiment Criteria

  • More than one possible outcome
  • Outcome cannot be predicted with certainty beforehand
  • Can be repeated under essentially the same conditions
  • All possible outcomes are known in advance

✅ Trial Essentials

  • One single performance of a random experiment
  • Produces exactly one outcome
  • Multiple trials form a composed experiment
  • Trials are typically independent and identically distributed
💼

9. Practical Applications

🏥 Clinical Trial: Testing a new drug.
• Random Experiment: The entire study where a patient receives either drug or placebo.
• Trial: One patient's treatment and outcome measurement.
• The experiment consists of many patient trials (e.g., 1,000 patients).
• After all trials, data analysis reveals the drug's effectiveness.
🏭 Quality Control: Testing light bulbs from a factory.
• Random Experiment: Testing a sample of 100 bulbs to estimate the defect rate.
• Trial: Testing one individual bulb (working or defective).
• The relative frequency of defects in the sample estimates the true defect probability for the entire production line.
