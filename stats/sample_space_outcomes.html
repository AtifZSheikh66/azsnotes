🎯 Sample Space & Sample Point

The fundamental building blocks: defining all possible outcomes of a random experiment

Probability Theory · Unit I · Core Concepts
📐

1. Introduction

Every random experiment produces an outcome. To study the experiment mathematically, we must first define the set of all possible outcomes. This set is the foundation upon which probability theory is built [citation:1][citation:3].

The sample space (denoted by \( \Omega \) or \( S \)) is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment [citation:1][citation:3][citation:7].

Each individual outcome within the sample space is called a sample point or elementary event [citation:1][citation:5].

📖

2. Formal Definitions

📌 Sample Space (\( \Omega \))

The sample space is the universal set containing every possible outcome of an experiment. It is denoted by \( \Omega \) (capital Omega) or \( S \). The sample space must satisfy two conditions [citation:5][citation:7]:

  • Collectively exhaustive — every possible outcome of the experiment is included
  • Mutually exclusive — no outcome can occur simultaneously with another

📍 Sample Point (\( \omega \))

A sample point (also called an outcome, elementary event, or atom) is a single element of the sample space. It is denoted by \( \omega \) (lowercase omega) [citation:1][citation:5][citation:7].

Mathematically, if \( \Omega \) is the sample space, then each \( \omega \in \Omega \) is a sample point [citation:1]. A sample point cannot be broken down into simpler outcomes — it is the most basic result of the experiment.

✍️

3. Mathematical Notation

Symbol Name Meaning Example
\( \Omega \) or \( S \) Sample space Set of all possible outcomes \( \Omega = \{H, T\} \) for a coin toss
\( \omega \) Sample point / Outcome Individual element of \( \Omega \) \( \omega = H \) (Heads)
\( |\Omega| \) or \( n(\Omega) \) Cardinality Number of sample points in \( \Omega \) \( |\Omega| = 6 \) for a die roll
\( P(\{\omega\}) \) Probability of a sample point Probability that specific outcome occurs \( P(\{H\}) = \frac{1}{2} \) for fair coin
💡 Important Distinction: In probability theory, \( p(x) \) refers to the probability of a sample point (individual outcome), while \( P(X) \) refers to the probability of an event (set of sample points) [citation:5].
🔢

4. Types of Sample Spaces

📊 Discrete Sample Space

A sample space is discrete if it contains a finite or countably infinite number of outcomes [citation:2][citation:7].

Examples:

  • Coin toss: \( \Omega = \{H, T\} \) (2 outcomes)
  • Die roll: \( \Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\} \) (6 outcomes)
  • Number of coin tosses until first head: \( \Omega = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\} \) (countably infinite)

For discrete spaces, probabilities are assigned to individual sample points, and for any event \( A \subseteq \Omega \) [citation:2][citation:5]:

\[ P(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} P(\{\omega\}) \]

📏 Continuous Sample Space

A sample space is continuous if it contains an uncountably infinite number of outcomes [citation:5][citation:7].

Examples:

  • Measuring temperature: \( \Omega = \{T : T \in \mathbb{R}, T \geq -273.15\} \)
  • Waiting time for a bus: \( \Omega = \{t : t \geq 0\} \)
  • Angle of a spinning pointer: \( \Omega = [0, 2\pi) \)

For continuous spaces, probabilities are assigned via a probability density function \( f(\omega) \), and [citation:5]:

\[ P(A) = \int_{\omega \in A} f(\omega) \, d\omega \]

5. Worked Examples

Example 1: Single Coin Toss

Experiment: Tossing a fair coin once.

Sample Space: \( \Omega = \{H, T\} \) [citation:2][citation:10]

Sample Points: \( \omega_1 = H \) (Heads), \( \omega_2 = T \) (Tails)

Cardinality: \( |\Omega| = 2 \)

Probabilities (fair coin): \( P(\{H\}) = \frac{1}{2} \), \( P(\{T\}) = \frac{1}{2} \)

Example 2: Rolling a Fair Die

Experiment: Rolling a standard six-sided die.

Sample Space: \( \Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\} \) [citation:1][citation:10]

Sample Points: Each integer from 1 to 6

Cardinality: \( |\Omega| = 6 \)

Probabilities (fair die): \( P(\{i\}) = \frac{1}{6} \) for \( i = 1, 2, \ldots, 6 \)

Probability of an even number (event): \( P(\{2,4,6\}) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} \) [citation:2]

Example 3: Two Coin Tosses

Experiment: Tossing a fair coin twice.

Sample Space (ordered outcomes): \( \Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\} \) [citation:1][citation:3][citation:10]

Cardinality: \( |\Omega| = 4 \)

Each sample point probability: \( P(\{HH\}) = P(\{HT\}) = P(\{TH\}) = P(\{TT\}) = \frac{1}{4} \)

Event "Exactly one head": \( A = \{HT, TH\} \)

Probability: \( P(A) = P(\{HT\}) + P(\{TH\}) = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} \)

Example 4: Rolling Two Dice

Experiment: Rolling two fair dice.

Sample Space (ordered pairs): \( \Omega = \{(i,j) : i,j \in \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\} \) [citation:1][citation:2]

Cardinality: \( |\Omega| = 6 \times 6 = 36 \)

Probability of each sample point: \( P(\{(i,j)\}) = \frac{1}{36} \) for all \( i,j \)

Event "Sum = 7": \( A = \{(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)\} \)

Probability: \( P(A) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6} \) [citation:2]

Example 5: Countably Infinite Sample Space

Experiment: Tossing a fair coin until the first head appears [citation:4][citation:9].

Sample Space: \( \Omega = \{H, TH, TTH, TTTH, TTTTH, \ldots\} \) [citation:4]

This is a countably infinite sample space — theoretically, we could toss tails forever (though with probability 0).

Individual probabilities: \( P(\{H\}) = \frac{1}{2} \), \( P(\{TH\}) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \), \( P(\{TTH\}) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{8} \), etc. [citation:4]

Verification: The sum of all probabilities is \( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = 1 \)

\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = \frac{1/2}{1 - 1/2} = 1 \]
🏗️

6. Constructing Sample Spaces

When constructing a sample space, there are often multiple valid approaches. The key principle: choose the representation that makes probability calculation easiest [citation:2].

Option 1: Detailed Space (Equally Likely Outcomes)

For two dice, we can record ordered pairs \((i,j)\). This gives 36 equally likely outcomes, making probability calculations straightforward [citation:2].

\[ \Omega_{\text{detailed}} = \{(1,1), (1,2), \ldots, (6,6)\}, \quad |\Omega| = 36 \]

Option 2: Summarized Space (Not Equally Likely)

For two dice, we could record only the sum \( i + j \). The sample space is \( \{2, 3, 4, \ldots, 12\} \), but outcomes are not equally likely — e.g., sum 7 has probability \( \frac{6}{36} \) while sum 2 has probability \( \frac{1}{36} \) [citation:2].

💡 Best Practice: When possible, construct a sample space where all outcomes are equally likely. This simplifies probability calculations significantly.
⚙️

7. The Sample Point Method

For discrete sample spaces, probability calculations follow a systematic approach called the sample point method [citation:2][citation:6]:

Steps of the Sample Point Method

  1. List the sample space — Determine all possible outcomes of the experiment: \( \Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2, \ldots\} \)
  2. Assign probabilities to each sample point — Ensure \( P(\{\omega_i\}) \geq 0 \) and \( \sum_i P(\{\omega_i\}) = 1 \)
  3. Express the event of interest — Write event \( A \) as a set of sample points: \( A \subseteq \Omega \)
  4. Sum the probabilities — \( P(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} P(\{\omega\}) \)
📖 Example Application: Three fair coin tosses [citation:2][citation:8].
Sample space: \( \Omega = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT\} \)
Each sample point probability: \( \frac{1}{8} \)
Event \( A \) = "exactly two heads" = \( \{HHT, HTH, THH\} \)
\( P(A) = \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{8} = \frac{3}{8} \)
🔗

8. Sample Points vs. Events

A sample point is an individual outcome — the most elementary result possible. An event is any subset of the sample space [citation:1][citation:5].

Sample Point \( \omega \)

  • Single element of \( \Omega \)
  • Denoted by lowercase \( \omega \)
  • Also called "outcome" or "elementary event"
  • Cannot be decomposed further

Event \( A \)

  • Subset of \( \Omega \) (any size, including 1)
  • Denoted by capital letters \( A, B, C \)
  • Can contain one or many sample points
  • Entire sample space \( \Omega \) is the certain event
\[ \text{If } \omega \in A \text{, then the occurrence of outcome } \omega \text{ implies event } A \text{ occurs} \] \[ \text{Every sample point is an event, but not every event is a sample point} \]

9. Practice Questions

Question 1: An experiment consists of selecting a card from a standard 52-card deck. What is the sample space? How many sample points does it contain?

Show Answer

\( \Omega = \{\text{Ace of Hearts}, 2\text{ of Hearts}, \ldots, \text{King of Spades}\} \). Number of sample points: \( |\Omega| = 52 \).

Question 2: An experiment consists of rolling a die and then tossing a coin. Describe the sample space.

Show Answer

\( \Omega = \{(1,H), (1,T), (2,H), (2,T), \ldots, (6,H), (6,T)\} \). Cardinality: \( 6 \times 2 = 12 \) equally likely outcomes.

Question 3: For the experiment of tossing a coin until a head appears, is the sample space finite or infinite?

Show Answer

The sample space is countably infinite: \( \Omega = \{H, TH, TTH, TTTH, \ldots\} \).

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Sample space \( \Omega \): The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment [citation:1][citation:3]
  • Sample point \( \omega \): A single outcome — an element of the sample space [citation:1][citation:5]
  • Discrete vs. Continuous: Discrete spaces have finite/countably infinite outcomes; continuous spaces have uncountably infinite outcomes [citation:7]
  • Sample Point Method: For discrete spaces, \( P(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} P(\{\omega\}) \) [citation:2][citation:5]
  • Events vs. Outcomes: Outcomes are individual results; events are sets of outcomes [citation:1][citation:3]
