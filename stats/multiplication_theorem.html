✖️ Multiplication Theorem of Probability

Computing the probability of the intersection of events — from two events to n events

Probability Theory · Unit II · Multiplication Theorem
📜

1. Introduction

The Multiplication Theorem of Probability (also called the Multiplication Rule) provides a method for computing the probability of the intersection of events using conditional probabilities. It is a fundamental tool that allows us to compute \( P(A \cap B) \) when we know the conditional probability \( P(A \mid B) \) and the marginal probability \( P(B) \), or vice versa.

This theorem is derived directly from the definition of conditional probability and does not assume independence of events. When events are independent, the multiplication rule simplifies to the product of the individual probabilities, but the general form works for any events.

🔢

2. Multiplication Theorem for Two Events

📌 Theorem (Multiplication Theorem for Two Events)

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A) \]

provided that \( P(A) > 0 \) and \( P(B) > 0 \).

The theorem states that the probability of the joint occurrence of two events equals the probability of the first event multiplied by the conditional probability of the second given the first (or the probability of the second multiplied by the conditional probability of the first given the second).

📝 Proof of Multiplication Theorem for Two Events:

Step 1 — Recall the definition of conditional probability:

By definition, for any events A and B with \( P(B) > 0 \),

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \]
Step 2 — Multiply both sides by \( P(B) \):
\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) \]
Step 3 — Alternative form:

Similarly, from the definition \( P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \) for \( P(A) > 0 \), we multiply both sides by \( P(A) \) to obtain:

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A) \]
Step 4 — Conclusion:

Thus, both forms are equivalent, and the theorem is proved directly from the definition of conditional probability.

📌 Corollary 2.1 (Product of Conditional Probabilities):
\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \]
This follows directly from the multiplication rule by dividing both sides by \( P(B) \).

📌 Corollary 2.2 (Chain Rule for Two Events):
\[ P(A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B \mid A)} \]
This allows us to compute \( P(A) \) if we know the joint probability and the conditional probability \( P(B \mid A) \).

📌 Corollary 2.3 (Symmetry of Intersection): The multiplication rule shows the symmetry \( P(A \cap B) = P(B \cap A) \) is preserved since both expressions yield the same result.
🔢🔢

3. Multiplication Theorem for n Events (Chain Rule)

📌 Theorem (General Multiplication Theorem for n Events)

\[ P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdot P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1}) \]

provided that all conditional probabilities are defined (i.e., each conditioning event has positive probability).

This is also known as the Chain Rule of Probability or the Product Rule for n Events.

📝 Proof of Multiplication Theorem for n Events (by Mathematical Induction):

Step 1 — Base Case (n = 2):

From the definition of conditional probability, we have already proved:

\[ P(A_1 \cap A_2) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \]

Thus, the base case holds.

Step 2 — Inductive Hypothesis:

Assume that the multiplication theorem holds for any collection of \( m \) events, where \( m \geq 2 \). That is:

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} A_i \right) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdot P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P(A_m \mid A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_{m-1}) \]
Step 3 — Inductive Step (prove for \( n = m+1 \) events):

Consider \( m+1 \) events \( A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_m, A_{m+1} \).

Let \( B = \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} A_i \). Then the intersection of all \( m+1 \) events can be written as:

\[ \bigcap_{i=1}^{m+1} A_i = B \cap A_{m+1} \]
Step 4 — Apply the multiplication theorem for two events to \( B \) and \( A_{m+1} \):
\[ P(B \cap A_{m+1}) = P(B) \cdot P(A_{m+1} \mid B) \]

provided \( P(B) > 0 \).

Step 5 — Apply the inductive hypothesis to \( P(B) \):
\[ P(B) = P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} A_i \right) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdot P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P(A_m \mid A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_{m-1}) \]
Step 6 — Substitute the expression for \( P(B) \):
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{m+1} A_i \right) = \left[ P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdots P(A_m \mid A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{m-1}) \right] \cdot P(A_{m+1} \mid A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_m) \]
Step 7 — Conclusion:

Therefore, the formula holds for \( n = m+1 \). By the principle of mathematical induction, the multiplication theorem holds for all positive integers \( n \geq 2 \).

\[ \boxed{P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) = P(A_1) \cdot \prod_{k=2}^{n} P\left( A_k \;\middle|\; \bigcap_{i=1}^{k-1} A_i \right)} \]
📌 Corollary 3.1 (Alternative Ordering): The multiplication theorem can be applied in any order. For any permutation \( \sigma \) of \( \{1,2,\ldots,n\} \),
\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) = P(A_{\sigma(1)}) \cdot P(A_{\sigma(2)} \mid A_{\sigma(1)}) \cdots P(A_{\sigma(n)} \mid A_{\sigma(1)} \cap \cdots \cap A_{\sigma(n-1)}) \]
This follows because the left-hand side is symmetric in the events, but the conditional expansions differ.

📌 Corollary 3.2 (Sequential Experiment Interpretation): For a sequence of dependent events, the probability of the entire sequence can be computed step by step using conditional probabilities.

📌 Corollary 3.3 (Chain Rule for Conditional Probabilities):
\[ P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1}) = \frac{P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n)}{P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1})} \]
∏

4. Compact Form Using Product Notation

📌 Compact Representation

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) = P(A_1) \prod_{i=2}^{n} P\left( A_i \;\middle|\; \bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1} A_j \right) \]

This concise form emphasizes that the joint probability is the product of the marginal probability of the first event and the subsequent conditional probabilities, each conditioned on all previous events.

📖 Example — Drawing Cards Without Replacement:

Find the probability of drawing 4 aces in a row from a standard deck of 52 cards without replacement.

Let \( A_i \) be the event that the i-th card drawn is an ace.

\[ P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3 \cap A_4) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdot P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdot P(A_4 \mid A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3) \]
\[ = \frac{4}{52} \cdot \frac{3}{51} \cdot \frac{2}{50} \cdot \frac{1}{49} = \frac{24}{6497400} = \frac{1}{270725} \approx 0.00000369 \]
🔗

5. Special Case: Independent Events

If events \( A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n \) are mutually independent, then the conditional probabilities simplify:

\[ P(A_i \mid A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_{i-1}) = P(A_i) \quad \text{for all } i \]

Therefore, the multiplication theorem reduces to:

\[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \]
💡 Important Note: The general multiplication theorem (without independence) is always true. The simplified product form \( \prod P(A_i) \) is only valid when the events are independent. Do not use the simplified form unless independence is explicitly stated or justified.

6. Worked Examples

Example 1: Balls from an Urn (Without Replacement)
An urn contains 5 red balls and 3 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Find the probability that both are red.

Solution: Let \( R_1 \) = "first ball is red", \( R_2 \) = "second ball is red".
\[ P(R_1) = \frac{5}{8} \]
\[ P(R_2 \mid R_1) = \frac{4}{7} \]
\[ P(R_1 \cap R_2) = P(R_1) \cdot P(R_2 \mid R_1) = \frac{5}{8} \cdot \frac{4}{7} = \frac{20}{56} = \frac{5}{14} \approx 0.3571 \]
Example 2: Selecting a Committee
A committee of 3 people is to be selected randomly from a group of 4 men and 3 women. Find the probability that all three selected are men.

Solution: Let \( M_1, M_2, M_3 \) be the events that the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd selected are men.
\[ P(M_1) = \frac{4}{7} \]
\[ P(M_2 \mid M_1) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} \]
\[ P(M_3 \mid M_1 \cap M_2) = \frac{2}{5} \]
\[ P(M_1 \cap M_2 \cap M_3) = \frac{4}{7} \cdot \frac{3}{6} \cdot \frac{2}{5} = \frac{24}{210} = \frac{4}{35} \approx 0.1143 \]
Example 3: Dependent Events (Rain Probability)
The probability of rain on Monday is 0.3. If it rains on Monday, the probability of rain on Tuesday is 0.8. If it does not rain on Monday, the probability of rain on Tuesday is 0.2. Find the probability that it rains on both days.

Solution: Let \( R_1 \) = "rain on Monday", \( R_2 \) = "rain on Tuesday".
\[ P(R_1) = 0.3, \quad P(R_2 \mid R_1) = 0.8 \]
\[ P(R_1 \cap R_2) = P(R_1) \cdot P(R_2 \mid R_1) = 0.3 \times 0.8 = 0.24 \]

7. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1 — Assuming Independence: Many students incorrectly assume \( P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) \) without checking independence. The general multiplication rule \( P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B \mid A) \) must be used unless independence is explicitly stated.

Misconception 2 — Reversing Conditional Probabilities: \( P(B \mid A) \) is not equal to \( P(A \mid B) \) in general. The multiplication rule shows that \( P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B \mid A) = P(B)P(A \mid B) \), but \( P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B)P(B)}{P(A)} \) — this is Bayes' theorem.

Misconception 3 — Order of Conditioning: In the chain rule, the order matters. Different orders yield different conditional probabilities, but the final product is the same because it equals \( P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) \).

8. Practice Problems

Problem 1: A bag contains 6 red balls and 4 blue balls. Three balls are drawn without replacement. What is the probability that all three are red?

Show Solution

\( P(R_1) = \frac{6}{10} \), \( P(R_2 \mid R_1) = \frac{5}{9} \), \( P(R_3 \mid R_1 \cap R_2) = \frac{4}{8} = \frac{1}{2} \).
\( P = \frac{6}{10} \cdot \frac{5}{9} \cdot \frac{4}{8} = \frac{120}{720} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.1667 \).

Problem 2: In a deck of 52 cards, what is the probability of drawing a king, then another king, without replacement?

Show Solution

\( P(K_1) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13} \), \( P(K_2 \mid K_1) = \frac{3}{51} = \frac{1}{17} \).
\( P = \frac{1}{13} \cdot \frac{1}{17} = \frac{1}{221} \approx 0.00452 \).

Problem 3: A factory produces items with defect rates: 2% from Machine A (60% of production) and 3% from Machine B (40% of production). What is the probability that a randomly selected item is defective? (Hint: Use Law of Total Probability, not multiplication theorem directly)

Show Solution

\( P(D) = P(D \mid A)P(A) + P(D \mid B)P(B) = 0.02 \times 0.6 + 0.03 \times 0.4 = 0.012 + 0.012 = 0.024 \)

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Multiplication Theorem for Two Events: \( P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) \)
  • General Multiplication Theorem (Chain Rule):
    \[ P\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right) = P(A_1) \prod_{k=2}^{n} P\left( A_k \;\middle|\; \bigcap_{i=1}^{k-1} A_i \right) \]
  • Derived directly from the definition of conditional probability — no independence assumption required
  • Proved by mathematical induction for n events
  • Special case (independent events): \( P(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \)
  • Applications: Sequential experiments, sampling without replacement, reliability analysis, decision trees
