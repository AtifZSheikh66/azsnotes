🧠 Bayes' Theorem

Updating probabilities in light of new evidence — the foundation of Bayesian inference

Probability Theory · Unit II · Bayes' Theorem
📜

1. Introduction

Bayes' Theorem (also called Bayes' Rule or Bayes' Law) is a fundamental result in probability theory that describes how to update the probability of a hypothesis based on observed evidence. It was first formulated by the Reverend Thomas Bayes (1701-1761) and later developed by Pierre-Simon Laplace.

Bayes' Theorem allows us to "reverse" conditional probabilities: given \(P(A \mid B)\), we can compute \(P(B \mid A)\) using prior knowledge. This is the foundation of Bayesian statistics, machine learning, medical diagnosis, and many other fields.

📐

2. Bayes' Theorem for Two Events

📌 Theorem (Bayes' Theorem — Two Events)

For any two events \(A\) and \(B\) with \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\),

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)} \]

Using the Law of Total Probability, this can be expanded as:

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) P(B)}{P(A \mid B)P(B) + P(A \mid B^c)P(B^c)} \]

📝 Proof:

Step 1 — Start with the definition of conditional probability:
\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \quad \text{and} \quad P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \]
Step 2 — Express \(P(A \cap B)\) from the second equation:
\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) \]
Step 3 — Substitute into the first equation:
\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)} \]
Step 4 — Expand \(P(A)\) using the Law of Total Probability:
\[ P(A) = P(A \mid B)P(B) + P(A \mid B^c)P(B^c) \]
Step 5 — Conclusion:
\[ \boxed{P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) P(B)}{P(A \mid B)P(B) + P(A \mid B^c)P(B^c)}} \]
📌 Corollary 2.1 (Odds Form):
\[ \frac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B^c \mid A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B)}{P(A \mid B^c)} \cdot \frac{P(B)}{P(B^c)} \]
Posterior odds = Likelihood ratio × Prior odds. 📌 Corollary 2.2 (Discrete Case): For a partition \(\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\}\),
\[ P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_j) P(B_j)} \]

4. Bayes' Theorem for a Partition (General Form)

📌 Theorem (Bayes' Theorem — General Form)

Let \(\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\}\) be a partition of the sample space \(\Omega\) with \(P(B_i) > 0\) for all \(i\). Then for any event \(A\) with \(P(A) > 0\),

\[ P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_j) P(B_j)} \]

📝 Proof:

Step 1 — By definition of conditional probability:
\[ P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B_i)}{P(A)} \]
Step 2 — Apply the multiplication rule:
\[ P(A \cap B_i) = P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i) \]
Step 3 — Apply the Law of Total Probability to \(P(A)\):
\[ P(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_j) P(B_j) \]
Step 4 — Substitute into Step 1:
\[ P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_j) P(B_j)} \]
Step 5 — Conclusion:
\[ \boxed{P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_j) P(B_j)}} \]
📌 Corollary 4.1 (Countable Partition): For a countably infinite partition,
\[ P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty} P(A \mid B_j) P(B_j)} \]
📌 Corollary 4.2 (Normalizing Constant): The denominator \(\sum P(A \mid B_j)P(B_j) = P(A)\) is the normalizing constant that ensures the posterior probabilities sum to 1.

5. Bayes' Theorem for Future Events (Predictive Distribution)

📌 Theorem (Bayesian Prediction — Posterior Predictive Distribution)

Let \(B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\) be a partition representing different hypotheses or states. After observing data \(A\), the probability of a future event \(F\) is given by:

\[ P(F \mid A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(F \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i \mid A) \]

This is the posterior predictive distribution — it averages the predictions of each hypothesis weighted by their posterior probabilities.

📝 Proof:

Step 1 — Apply the Law of Total Probability conditional on A:
\[ P(F \mid A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(F \cap B_i \mid A) \]
Step 2 — Use the chain rule for conditional probability:
\[ P(F \cap B_i \mid A) = P(F \mid B_i \cap A) \cdot P(B_i \mid A) \]
Step 3 — Note that \(P(F \mid B_i \cap A) = P(F \mid B_i)\) if future event \(F\) is independent of past \(A\) given \(B_i\):
\[ P(F \mid A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(F \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i \mid A) \]
Step 4 — Conclusion:
\[ \boxed{P(F \mid A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(F \mid B_i) P(B_i \mid A)} \]
📖 Example — Predicting a Future Event:
Two coins: Coin 1 is fair (\(P(H)=0.5\)). Coin 2 is biased (\(P(H)=0.8\)). A coin is chosen with equal probability and tossed, showing Heads. What is the probability that the next toss also shows Heads?

Solution: Let \(B_1\) = "fair coin", \(B_2\) = "biased coin". Prior: \(P(B_1)=P(B_2)=0.5\). Likelihood: \(P(H \mid B_1)=0.5\), \(P(H \mid B_2)=0.8\).
First, compute posteriors:
\[ P(B_1 \mid H) = \frac{0.5 \times 0.5}{0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.8 \times 0.5} = \frac{0.25}{0.65} = \frac{5}{13} \approx 0.3846 \]
\[ P(B_2 \mid H) = 1 - \frac{5}{13} = \frac{8}{13} \approx 0.6154 \]
Then, predictive probability for next head:
\[ P(H_2 \mid H_1) = 0.5 \times \frac{5}{13} + 0.8 \times \frac{8}{13} = \frac{2.5}{13} + \frac{6.4}{13} = \frac{8.9}{13} \approx 0.6846 \]
📌 Corollary 5.1 (Sequential Updating): Bayes' theorem can be applied sequentially as new data arrives:
\[ P(B \mid A_1, A_2) = \frac{P(A_2 \mid B, A_1) P(B \mid A_1)}{P(A_2 \mid A_1)} \]
📌 Corollary 5.2 (Posterior as a New Prior): The posterior distribution from one experiment becomes the prior for the next experiment.

6. Worked Examples

📖 Example 1: Medical Test (Classic)
A disease affects 1 in 1000 people. A test has 99% sensitivity and 95% specificity. If a person tests positive, what is the probability they have the disease?

Solution: Let D = "has disease", T = "tests positive".
Prior: \(P(D) = 0.001\), \(P(D^c) = 0.999\)
Likelihood: \(P(T \mid D) = 0.99\), \(P(T \mid D^c) = 0.05\)
\[ P(D \mid T) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.05 \times 0.999} = \frac{0.00099}{0.00099 + 0.04995} = \frac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx 0.01944 \]
So only about 1.94% of positive tests actually indicate disease — a classic result showing the importance of base rates.
📖 Example 2: Email Spam Classification
In a set of emails, 20% are spam. The word "free" appears in 80% of spam emails and in 10% of non-spam emails. If an email contains the word "free", what is the probability it is spam?

Solution: Let S = "spam", F = "contains 'free'".
\(P(S) = 0.2\), \(P(F \mid S) = 0.8\), \(P(F \mid S^c) = 0.1\)
\[ P(S \mid F) = \frac{0.8 \times 0.2}{0.8 \times 0.2 + 0.1 \times 0.8} = \frac{0.16}{0.16 + 0.08} = \frac{0.16}{0.24} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.6667 \]

7. Uses of Bayes' Theorem

📊 Bayesian Statistics

Foundation of modern Bayesian inference. Combines prior beliefs with data to obtain posterior distributions.

🩺 Medical Diagnosis

Interpreting test results correctly, accounting for disease prevalence and test accuracy.

🤖 Machine Learning

Naive Bayes classifiers, Bayesian networks, and probabilistic graphical models.

📧 Spam Filtering

Classifying emails as spam or ham based on word frequencies.

⚖️ Legal Reasoning

Evaluating evidence and updating beliefs in court cases (avoiding prosecutor's fallacy).

🔬 Scientific Research

Updating hypotheses as new experimental data becomes available.

⚠️

8. Limitations of Bayes' Theorem

🎯 Prior Specification

Bayes' theorem requires a prior distribution. In the absence of objective prior information, selecting an appropriate prior can be subjective and controversial.

🧮 Computational Complexity

For complex models with many parameters, computing the denominator (marginal likelihood) can be computationally intensive or intractable.

📉 Sensitivity to Priors

Results can be sensitive to the choice of prior, especially with limited data. This can lead to different conclusions from different priors.

🔢 Disagreement on Priors

Different experts may have different prior beliefs, leading to different posterior probabilities even with the same data.

📏 Proper Scoring

For continuous parameters, defining a proper prior that adequately represents ignorance can be challenging (e.g., Jeffreys priors).

📊 Data Dependence

Assumes the likelihood model is correctly specified. Model misspecification can lead to poor inference.

⚠️

9. Common Fallacies

Prosecutor's Fallacy: Confusing \(P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{innocence})\) with \(P(\text{innocence} \mid \text{evidence})\). Bayes' theorem shows these are generally different.
Base Rate Fallacy: Ignoring prior probabilities (base rates) when interpreting conditional probabilities. The medical test example demonstrates this.
Confusion of the Inverse: Assuming \(P(A \mid B) = P(B \mid A)\). This is only true when \(P(A) = P(B)\).

10. Practice Problems

Problem 1: In a city, 2% of drivers are drunk. A breathalyzer test has 98% accuracy (correctly identifies drunk drivers 98% of the time and correctly identifies sober drivers 98% of the time). If a driver tests positive, what is the probability they are actually drunk?

Show Solution

\(P(D)=0.02\), \(P(T \mid D)=0.98\), \(P(T \mid D^c)=0.02\). \(P(D \mid T) = \frac{0.98 \times 0.02}{0.98 \times 0.02 + 0.02 \times 0.98} = \frac{0.0196}{0.0196 + 0.0196} = 0.5\) (50%)

Problem 2: Prove that \(P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B)P(B)}{P(A \mid B)P(B) + P(A \mid B^c)P(B^c)}\) from first principles.

Show Solution

Start from definition: \(P(B \mid A) = P(A \cap B)/P(A)\). Then \(P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B)\). Expand \(P(A)\) using total probability: \(P(A) = P(A \mid B)P(B) + P(A \mid B^c)P(B^c)\). Substituting gives the result.

Problem 3: Three urns: Urn A has 2 red, 3 blue; Urn B has 4 red, 1 blue; Urn C has 1 red, 4 blue. An urn is chosen at random, then a ball is drawn and found to be red. What is the probability it came from Urn B?

Show Solution

\(P(U_i) = 1/3\), \(P(R \mid U_A)=2/5=0.4\), \(P(R \mid U_B)=4/5=0.8\), \(P(R \mid U_C)=1/5=0.2\). Denominator = \(0.4+0.8+0.2)/3 = 1.4/3\). \(P(U_B \mid R) = (0.8/3) / (1.4/3) = 0.8/1.4 = 4/7 ≈ 0.5714\).

📝 Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Bayes' Theorem (Two Events): \(P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B)P(B)}{P(A)}\)
  • General Form (Partition): \(P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i)P(B_i)}{\sum_{j} P(A \mid B_j)P(B_j)}\)
  • Future Events (Posterior Predictive): \(P(F \mid A) = \sum_i P(F \mid B_i) P(B_i \mid A)\)
  • Bayes' theorem allows us to: Update beliefs (prior → posterior); reverse conditional probabilities; make predictions
  • Uses: Medical diagnosis, spam filtering, machine learning, scientific inference, legal reasoning
  • Limitations: Requires prior specification; computationally intensive for complex models; sensitivity to priors
